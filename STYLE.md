# How the stories are written

The one place for everything learned about the style of the stories.
Read it before writing or changing a story. Add to it every time Chris
reacts to one, in his words where possible, with the date.

There are two layers, and a story needs both:

1. **General: this file.** What every story of the period does, what the
   magazines' house styles were, and the modern habits to keep out.
2. **Author-specific: the writer's file in `personas/`.** That writer's
   voice rules, plus a "Period notes" section written after reading real
   stories from their own magazines. Tugwell, Trembath, Staniforth and
   Kenworthy have theirs. The other six do not, and must before they
   write.

The evidence behind the general rules (what was read, the counts, the
gaps) is in `research/period-style.md`.

## Who decides

- Inside a story, the writer and the period decide. Chris's general
  rules for writing (short words, no em dashes, Orwell) do not apply.
  "I don't really want my general md affecting these." (11 Nov 2026)
- "These need to be believable as historical objects." (11 Nov 2026)
- Everything written in the present day still follows his house style:
  biographies, About page, notes, commit messages.

## Before writing

1. Read the writer's file in `personas/` and the comparison in
   `personas/README.md`.
2. Read real stories from the magazine the story is meant to have
   appeared in. `research/period-style.md` says how, and which magazines
   are still unread.
3. Check any fact marked unverified before leaning on it.

## Set it as the magazine would

House style belongs to the magazine, not the writer.

- ***Chambers's Journal*, 1899–1900:** single quotation marks, double
  inside; "Mr", "Mrs", "Dr" with no full point; "gray"; "-ise";
  "to-day", "to-night", "any one", "every one", "for ever";
  "drawing-room", "arm-chair", "cocoa-nut"; but "bedroom", "upstairs",
  "bookcase", "firelight". Long unspaced dashes. Stories signed, 3,200 to
  4,300 words, complete in one number. First word or two in capitals.
- ***Temple Bar*, 1890–91:** double quotation marks; "Mrs." with a point.
- ***Pearson's Magazine*, 1898:** double quotation marks; "Mr." with a
  point; "grey"; "anyone"; "to-day"; "-ise". Short paragraphs, about
  two-fifths speech, far fewer semicolons than *Chambers's* (5 or 6 per
  1,000 words), and hard endings.
- Other magazines: not read yet. Read them first.

## What the prose of the time does

- Sentences are not especially long: the middle one in *Chambers's* is
  13 or 14 words, in *Temple Bar* 18.
- About one semicolon and one dash to every hundred words.
- Feelings are stated. The author may speak to the reader.
- Stories open on scenery or on someone's circumstances, are heavily
  plotted, and close by saying what became of everyone.
- They carry slack: a description of the town, a digression, a foolish
  opinion, a pious remark.

## Tells to keep out

**How to use this list.** It is not a blanket ban. Applied equally to
all ten writers it would press them into one careful plain style, and
the experiment would fail from the other side. Two rules, both agreed
with Chris on 12 Nov 2026:

1. **A high bar, with a reason.** Many of these habits are true to the
   period and are also mine. A reader today hears me before they hear
   1899. So each one is rationed hard, and each use needs a reason that
   can be stated. "The dog I made myself, in the train between Sheffield
   and Chesterfield" earns its place: it lands on something specific and
   absurd, and that drop is the comic papers' main instrument.
2. **Ask which writer it is wrong for.** The short paragraph and the
   sting are wrong for Tugwell and right for Staniforth. Bathos is his
   tool and nobody else's.

**The test behind most of them.** Chris: the construction "signalled
importance, but as I got to the end of the clause I realised I wasn't
sure what was important, went back to re-read and concluded there was
nothing important there. This leaves the reader with either an
impression of synthetic writing or a human doing style over substance."
So: never let the shape of a sentence promise weight that its content
does not carry. If a sentence signals that something matters, the thing
that matters must be in it, and nameable.

Chris found "The Reader" "a little modern or classic Claude" and
confirmed this list ("that's pretty much the list", 11 Nov 2026).

- **The one-line kicker.** A short paragraph that lands a point and
  stops. Fold it into its neighbour.
- **The aphorism**, as a habit. See below for the exception.
- **Lists of three.**
- **"Not this, but that."** It is the empty kind that is the tell. Where
  the contrast is the point, it can stay: Chris minded "They were not
  looking at the dead man... They were looking at the water" less,
  because "at least here it's meaningful that they weren't looking at
  the obvious thing." (12 Nov 2026)
- **Motifs that all pay off.** Leave some details doing nothing.
- **Unbroken restraint.** Let someone be foolish, sentimental or devout.
- **Modern idiom.** "A kind of statement."
- **The fronted punch sentence.** "Two of the houses I never set foot
  in." Chris, 12 Nov 2026: "It may be period accurate, I don't know, but
  it is a consistent tell of your natural speaking voice. It reads like
  someone trying to turn a straightforward sentence into a more
  interesting one, and failing." Put the sentence in its plain order.
- **The tail that adds weight but no content.** A last clause that
  sounds like a point being made and is not one. Two kinds so far, both
  from "The Last Car but One", both caught by Chris on 12 Nov 2026.
  - The vague one: "...the only one that I ever got for nothing, and
    have never been able to do anything with since." Chris: "It feels
    like an attempt to add profundity to a sentence but without any real
    weight. And it can be confusing. Reading it cold, I find myself
    consuming it and then going back to it to clarify it." It also
    contradicted the story, in which he is paid.
  - The echo: "...and fifty people were standing round to watch it do
    so." Chris: "That final leg actually only contains one new thing
    (the number of the crowd) and then repeats the fact that the crowd
    were looking. It feels like an important or profound point being
    added, but the content isn't."
  Not every hint is this fault. Chris did not mind "and then I went to
  bed, and I may say that I slept", which is short, clear and paid off
  later. He did want "I should be an easier man to-day" and "the only
  money I ever had out of a ghost that I did not have to work for"
  changed.
  The test for the end of any sentence: what does this clause tell the
  reader that they did not already have? If one fact, put the fact where
  it belongs and stop. If nothing, cut it.
- **The balanced sentence that ends on nothing.** "I got what I could
  that night from the constable, which was the name and address, and
  from the conductor, which was nothing at all." Chris, 12 Nov 2026: "I
  recognise that you want to give the sense that there was little to go
  on, but ending on the 'nothing at all' is a kind of dying fall into a
  void." The matched halves are there for the rhythm, and the rhythm
  lands on an empty word. Say who gave what in plain order, and let the
  sentence end on something: a person, an action, a thing.
  Two exceptions, both agreed. Where the emptiness is the point, it
  stays: "Hobson had none" (a man with no answer). And a drop that lands
  on something specific is a joke and not this fault.
- **The announcement.** "I went to that inquest in a thoroughly
  comfortable frame of mind, and it is only right that I should tell you
  what was in it." Chris, 12 Nov 2026: "What is meant by 'what was in
  it'? In the inquest? In his frame of mind?" A clause that announces
  what is about to be said, with a loose "it" for the reader to puzzle
  over. The next sentence was already doing the job. Cut the
  announcement and let the sentence that follows speak.
  On reflection he put it down to the phrase itself: "in it" is "a
  strange way to refer to 'what was in my frame of mind'".
- **Asides to the reader, as a tic.** Chris asked for Staniforth to talk
  to the reader. The rewrite had thirteen asides ("I may say", "I must
  tell you", "you will want to know") in under 4,000 words, and several
  were announcements. Agreed 12 Nov 2026: ration them. Keep the ones
  that admit something ("I will confess that I had a hand in the wording
  of the bill"), set out his stance, or mark a change of form. Cut the
  ones that only clear the throat. It now has eight.
- **A plain thing said in a tortuous way.** "I began my report of it
  with a certain sentence; and as I have not been able to better that
  sentence in thirteen years, I shall begin with it again." Chris,
  12 Nov 2026: "The idea is fine, it's just randomly complex." It became
  "This is how my report of it began."
- **The narrator vouching for the writing.** The same sentence claimed
  that what followed could not be bettered. Chris: "is it really so good
  he couldn't improve it? ... the first sentence just says there were
  people in the car and no one on the top deck." Do not have a narrator
  tell the reader that a line is fine, neat or beyond improving. If it
  is good the reader will see it, and if it is ordinary the claim shows
  it up.
- **A figure that breaks when pushed.** "I speak as a manufacturer
  speaks of his own goods" was meant to say he knows ghosts are faked.
  But goods are real, so it says the opposite. Chris caught it and
  offered a conjuror who knows his own tricks. Test every comparison by
  asking what else it implies.

Quick check: no paragraph of narration under nine words.

**These are not the same for every writer.** Staniforth writes for the
popular press, where the short paragraph and the sting in the last line
are the house manner, and real *Pearson's* stories are full of both.
For him they stay. Chris: "Don't worry about the style - go with what
you have." (11 Nov 2026) The list above is for the writers whose
magazines did not do it.

## The first two stories, read against this list

On 12 Nov 2026 "The Reader" and "The Diary at Direction Island", both
written before most of the list existed, were read against it. Chris
agreed the calls.

- "The Reader": six lines changed. One claim was untrue to the story
  ("the one thing which she ever said upon the subject", when she speaks
  of it again at breakfast). Two tails went, and two phrases the reader
  had to decode were said plainly. Four lines of the same shape were
  kept, each for a reason: it states the story's subject, it lands on
  something specific, it is a servant hinting in character, or it marks
  a real gap.
- "The Diary at Direction Island": two tails cut. It was much the
  cleaner of the two, which fits how Chris and the detector took it.
  Trembath's two announcements stay ("I am particular as to these dates,
  because a great deal hangs by them"): both are true, and fussing over
  what matters is who he is.
- A check worth keeping from this: **is the sentence true to the rest of
  the story?** Twice now a line written for its sound has contradicted
  the plot.

## What Chris has liked

- He loved the first two "for their delicacy", and then asked for "a
  more traditionally scary one". So the set should vary in how hard it
  frightens, and delicate is not the only register he wants.

- The structure and the idea of "The Reader": "well structured and a
  lovely idea." (11 Nov 2026)
- **One good aphorism can stay.** He asked for "a vicarage belongs to
  the vicar" back after the rewrite cut it. My reading: a line like that
  is welcome once, where it is the writer's own subject. It is the habit
  he objects to.
- **The period ending over the modern one.** The rewrite of "The Reader"
  swapped a dry joke of a last line for a reflection on mercy to the
  very old. "Keep the new ending - I like it!" My reading: when the
  period's way and the modern way differ at the close, choose the
  period's, sentiment included.
- He said yes when asked whether narration (first or third person) was
  being thought about. It is fixed per writer: see `CLAUDE.md`.

- **A teller with a trade and a stake.** For Staniforth he liked the idea
  of a cynic who has faked ghosts for a living and cannot explain this
  one, who speaks straight to the reader, and who is told apart from the
  other writers "more in terms of vocabulary than position". "The voice
  is good." (12 Nov 2026)

- **A figure that is the writer's own.** "He gave his evidence as though
  every word of it were coming out of his own pocket." Chris, 12 Nov
  2026: "I actually like this sentence. It's a nice, characterful
  detail." It works because it holds when pushed (a man paying out his
  own money is slow and gives no more than he must) and because it is in
  Staniforth's coin: he puts everything in terms of money.

## What Chris has not liked

- **Detachment.** On rereading "The Last Car but One" (12 Nov 2026) he
  found it modern, and "detached in a way that makes sense superficially
  ('he's a reporter!') but doesn't quite ring true for me for this
  period and crucially leaves the story (which has a solid, scary idea)
  too antiseptic and unaffecting." His instinct: the narrator should talk
  directly to the reader, as one of life's cynics who cannot quite
  explain this one away, and be told apart from the others "more in
  terms of vocabulary than position".
- My reading, to test: a story told as a bare record gives the reader
  nobody to be frightened with. Fear needs a teller who has something at
  stake. A neutral reporter is also wrong for the 1890s, when popular
  journalism was personal, chatty and opinionated. And I measured the
  symptom (sentences half the length of real *Pearson's* ones) and then
  kept the form that caused it.

Staniforth's first version had sentences far shorter than real *Pearson's* ones, and read as modern. The rewrite matches them. All three of ours are light on dashes. Tugwell's and Trembath's middle
sentences are now nearly the same length, so what separates them is
person, paragraph length and how much speech there is. Watch that the
next writer differs on more than one count.

Kenworthy's first draft came out with the same middle sentence as
Tugwell's and the same count of semicolons, and with the jokes reported
in the teller's words. It read like the others with a flat cap on. The
second pass broke the compound sentences into spoken ones and gave the
jokes back to the men who made them, in dialect. Lesson: measure the
draft against the other stories before showing it, not only against
the real magazine.

## What a detector made of them

On 11 November 2026 Chris ran the opening of each published story
through Pangram, a tool that guesses whether text was written by a
machine. It is a smoke alarm, not a judge, and 180 words is a small
sample. The target is still a believable object of 1900, not a score.

- "The Diary at Direction Island" (Trembath): 100% human.
- "The Reader" (Tugwell), the opening as first published, before the
  rewrite: 100% AI. The rewritten opening has not been tested.
- A real passage, the opening of "The Chaperon" by R. Ramsay
  (*Chambers's Journal*, January 1900): 100% human. So the tool does
  recognise the genuine article, at least once.

Chris's view, in his words: "my aim is NOT to explicitly trick Pangram",
and "I don't fully trust Pangram, but I do see it as a positive sign
that a persona combined with attention to historic writing styles might
outfox something like Pangram." He is not concerned about the result for
"The Reader". So: never write to the detector. Note what it says when he
offers it, and no more.

My guesses at why, to be tested and not yet rules:

- Trembath is a plain man giving evidence. He hedges, interrupts himself
  and tacks on afterthoughts ("is thought, I know, to be a poor thing").
  His sentences trail. Tugwell's narrator is poised and ironic, with
  balanced clauses that each close neatly, and that poise is what a
  model produces by default when asked for good prose.
- Every detail in the opening of "The Reader" is well chosen and a
  little funny (the perambulator, the foot-rule). Trembath's opening
  has throat-clearing in it that does no work.
- First person in a working voice may simply be further from a model's
  habits than polished third person.

If these hold, the general lesson is to allow clumsiness: afterthoughts,
hedges, a sentence that says a thing twice. And Tugwell's irony, which
is her whole voice, is the hardest of the ten to make sound unmachined.

One thing the two tests suggest: the story written after reading the
real magazines passed, and the opening written before the rewrite did
not. That is two samples, and proves little, but it points the same way
as everything else in this file.
