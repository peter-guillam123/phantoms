IN THE month of June last I addressed a letter to the Secretary of a northern Society of Antiquaries, with whose Council I have enjoyed a polite if infrequent correspondence for some twelve years, advising them to remove from their museum a small collection of Roman-British objects presented to them in the autumn of 1902, and to restore the whole of it to the ground from which it was taken. I did not expect my advice to be followed; nor, to speak plainly, could I have blamed any body of sensible men for declining to act upon a letter which offered no archaeological reason for so singular a proposal. The Secretary replied with great civility that the collection had already been catalogued, mounted, and placed on view in the upper gallery, where it had attracted favourable notice from several gentlemen of the county, and that the Council saw no sufficient ground for disturbing an arrangement so satisfactory to the late donor's family. I did not write again. I have instead set down the circumstances of my acquaintance with those objects, not in the hope of altering the Council's mind—for even were they to alter it to-morrow, the remedy is no longer in their hands—but as evidence of nothing, or of as little as a man may reasonably ask his readers to believe.

My own small province of study lies among the humbler monuments of antiquity, and particularly among those sheets of thin lead, known to scholars as *defixiones*, upon which the ancients were accustomed to scratch their grievances against a neighbour or a thief before rolling them up, piercing them with an iron nail, and consigning them to a grave or a spring. It is a narrow field, and one that does not often bring a man into the newspapers; yet it has this advantage, that whenever a piece of folded lead is turned up in a British excavation, the finder is very apt to send for me before he attempts to unroll it with his penknife. So it was that in the second week of August 1902—just after the postponed Coronation had at last been got through in London, and while the flags were still hanging damp in the railway stations—I received a letter from a Mr Thomas Forster of Harlow Rigg, in Northumberland, inviting me to spend a week at his house and examine eleven such tablets which had come out of a well upon his property.

I knew Mr Forster's name slightly from the proceedings of the local societies, to which he contributed money more often than papers. He was the only son of a Tyneside coalowner who had bought a stretch of fellside along the line of the Roman Wall in the sixties, and had left his children very comfortably off. Mr Forster himself was a man of fifty, stoutly built, with a reddish beard trimmed close to the jaw and the loud, decisive manner of one who has never had to wait for a second opinion. He had been educated at no university, a circumstance which he mentioned twice during our first half-hour together, as though it were a qualification for independent judgment; and he had spent the previous two summers employing a dozen local men to clear the remains of a mile-castle that stood on the southern slope of his land, about three miles west of the better-known station at Carrawburgh.

Harlow Rigg was a square, gray house of local sandstone, built about the year 1840 in a style that seemed to resent the weather without quite succeeding in keeping it out. Mr Forster lived there with his elder sister, Miss Forster, a tall, silent lady of perhaps fifty-five who wore a black silk apron at dinner and never spoke unless she were directly addressed, and not always then. She had a habit of folding her napkin into a narrow strip and laying it beside her plate before the pudding was brought in, which I found mildly distracting; though I dare say it was merely the custom of the house. The dining-room itself was hung with steel engravings of the collieries from which the family fortune had been drawn, together with a very bad oil portrait of the late Mr Forster holding a rolled map of the Tyne.

'I don't pretend to your Greek and Latin, Mr—' Forster said to me over the soup on the evening of my arrival, 'and I've no patience with the fellows from Oxford who come up here for a fortnight and tell us Severus built the Wall because they read it in a book. I've dug into the footings myself. It's Hadrian's work from end to end, and any mason who uses his eyes can see it.'

I murmured something to the effect that the question of Severus's repairs was still much debated, and that the evidence of the inscriptions was not yet wholly conclusive; at which he laughed and told me I spoke just like a man who had never held a spade. He was not an unkindly host, for all his bluster; he kept an excellent cellar of claret, which he drank in rather larger quantities than I am used to, and he had gone to the trouble of having a small table placed by the window of my bedroom with a good lamp and a supply of blotting-paper in case I should wish to make notes at night.

The following morning was gray and close, with a low mist clinging to the whinstone crags to the north, and after a substantial breakfast Mr Forster led me out along the cart-track toward the mile-castle. The ground rose steadily for half a mile through rough pasture where a few black-faced sheep were grazing, and then flattened out into a narrow shelf overlooking the valley. Here the line of the Wall was plain enough, though its facing-stones had been robbed centuries ago to build the field-dykes; and here, set against its southern face, lay the rectangular enclosure of the mile-castle, measuring sixty-two feet from east to west and fifty-five from north to south.

What caught my eye before I had even stepped over the threshold of the south gate was the course of the great Wall itself. Directly behind the northern rampart of the mile-castle, the line of the barrier made a distinct, deliberate bend toward the north—a deflection of eleven feet, as I afterwards measured it with Forster's tape—before resuming its straight run across the moor. I stopped and pointed to it.

'You see that?' Forster said, with the pride of a man showing off a prize heifer. 'Not one of the books mentions it. They all draw it straight as a ruler on the Ordnance map.'

'Why does it bend?' I asked.

'To take in the well,' he replied, and led me through the gateway.

Now, any one who has given even a passing attention to the stations and castella of the Roman Wall knows that a well inside a mile-castle is a thing hitherto unknown. The mile-castles were small guard-posts, designed to hold perhaps thirty or forty men on garrison duty; they depended for their water upon neighbouring burns or cisterns outside the ramparts. Yet there, in the north-east corner of the enclosure, not six feet from the inner face of the deflected Wall, was a circular shaft four feet and two inches in diameter, steined with rough-dressed sandstone blocks to a depth of some fourteen feet, and surrounded by a low coping of worn flagstones. The water stood about three feet below the lip, dark and still, with a faint greenish scum collected against the leeward stones.

'It was choked to the brim with rubbish when we began,' Forster said, tapping the coping with his ash stick. 'Tumbled masonry, sheep bones, a couple of old cart-wheels—the usual muck. It took us five weeks to get to the bottom, and the pump had to be kept going day and night.'

I stood looking down into the shaft, and then back at the bend in the northern wall. 'Mr Forster,' I said, 'the Wall was not built to protect a well that the garrison dug. The well was here before the Wall was laid out. The Roman engineers shifted their line eleven feet to bring it inside the enclosure.'

He stared at me for a moment, and then gave a short bark of laughter. 'That's a bold guess for a man who's been on the ground ten minutes. What makes you think so?'

'The steining,' I said, pointing to the lower courses visible beneath the clear edge of the water. 'The upper three feet are Roman ashlar, roughly re-set; below that, the stones are unmortared field-cobbles, laid dry in a fashion much older than the second century. And you will observe that the floor of the mile-castle's barrack-room on the east side has been cramped by nearly four feet to leave a passage round the coping. They built around something they found already there.'

Forster did not quite like having his own discovery explained to him so quickly, and he changed the subject by calling out to a man who was sitting on an upturned barrow beside the west wall, eating bread and cheese from a red handkerchief.

'Dodd!' he shouted. 'Bring the key to the shed, will you?'

The man rose slowly, folded his handkerchief, and came toward us. He was a thick-set fellow of about forty, with the gray, powdery skin of a man who has spent his youth in the sandstone quarries, and a pair of very pale eyes set deep under heavy brows. He touched his cap to me without speaking, unlocked a corrugated-iron tool-shed that Forster had erected outside the south gate, and stood aside while we went in.

The finds from the well were laid out on two long trestle-tables covered with coarse sacking. It was a remarkable haul, though on a smaller scale than the famous deposit emptied by Mr John Clayton from Coventina's Well at Carrawburgh in 1876. Forster had numbered everything with neat paper tickets, and he rattled off the totals as we walked down the line: one thousand three hundred and six coins, mostly small brass of the third and fourth centuries, though running as early as Vespasian; three small sandstone altars, none of them more than fourteen inches high, and all three entirely devoid of lettering; a quantity of bronze hairpins, two enamelled fibulae, a broken bone comb, and a small pewter cup crushed flat. At the far end of the second table lay a heap of smooth white quartz pebbles, perhaps two bushels of them.

'Those came from the very bottom,' Forster said, picking one up and rolling it between his palm and thumb. 'A bed of them, nine inches thick, under the mud. There's no quartz like that on this ridge; the nearest place you'll find it is in the bed of the Black Burn, half a mile down the valley. Some one carried them up here by the basketful.'

'A lining,' I said. 'To make the water show bright, or to mark the floor of the basin. It is not uncommon in British springs.'

'And here,' he added, moving to a small wooden tray beside the pebbles, 'are your leaden friends. Eleven of them. I haven't touched them since Dodd washed the mud off.'

I bent over the tray. The tablets were small, most of them no larger than a playing-card, rolled tightly into cylinders about the thickness of a man's little finger. Ten of them had been pierced right through the roll by square-shanked iron nails, now rusted to shapeless lumps of orange scale. The eleventh, which lay a little apart from the rest, was slightly larger, of a paler, purer lead, and had no nail-hole at all; it had been folded flat in three, like a letter, rather than rolled.

'I'll have them carried up to the house for you after lunch,' Forster said. 'You can work on them in the library, where the light is decent. Oh—and before we go, you ought to see my little joke.'

He reached up to a shelf above the trestle-table and lifted down a large glass sweet-jar, such as one sees in a village shop. It was about a third full of modern bronze pennies, all of them dull and tarnished with mud. Tied around the neck of the jar was a luggage-label on which Forster had written in his bold, sloping hand: *Offerings to the Genius Loci, July 1902.*

'What are these?' I asked.

'My workmen's wages, or part of them,' Forster said, grinning. 'Dodd here came to me a month ago and told me that every man on the job was dropping a penny into the well first thing in the morning before he picked up his shovel. I didn't believe him at first, so I went down early one Tuesday and watched from behind the dyke. Sure enough, in they filed, one after another, and *plop* went a penny from every pocket. When I asked Dodd what the foolery meant, he wouldn't give me a straight answer—said only that it was a dry job, and the men liked to wet it. Well, I wasn't going to have good English copper mixed up with my Roman coins, so the last day we pumped her out I had Dodd fish the lot of them up and put them in this jar. There's four shillings and ninepence there. And do you know what happened? The next morning, every man jack of them packed his tools and walked off the job. All except Dodd, who's got too much sense to throw up thirty shillings a week for a superstition.'

I glanced at Dodd, who was standing in the doorway of the shed with his hands in his pockets. His face was entirely without expression.

'Is that so, Dodd?' I asked him. 'Why did the men leave?'

Dodd looked past my shoulder at the glass jar on the table. 'They'd had enough of the place, sir,' he said quietly.

'And you hadn't?'

'I'm a single man, sir, and the quarry's closed.' He stepped back into the gray daylight to let us pass, and locked the shed door behind us. As we walked back toward the house, Forster took a coin from his waistcoat pocket and pressed it into my hand. It was a worn silver denarius of Antoninus Pius, with the figure of Britannia seated on the reverse. 'A keepsake,' he said. 'There are twenty more like it in the trays, and I've no use for duplicates. Put it in your pocket.'

I thanked him, though I am not fond of accepting antiquities from a site that has not yet been properly published, and slipped the coin into my fob.

The work of unrolling lead tablets that have lain in wet earth for sixteen hundred years is slow and requires a steady hand. I spent the whole of that afternoon and the greater part of the next day in Mr Forster's library, working with a bowl of warm water, a fine camel-hair brush, and a bone paper-knife. Lead of that age becomes brittle and crystalline; if one hurries it, the sheet cracks along the fold and the letters flake away into gray dust. Miss Forster came in once to ask if I required a fire, and seeing that I did not, withdrew without another word.

By four o'clock on the second afternoon I had unrolled and read the ten nailed tablets. They were, on the whole, very much what I had expected, and closely resembled the well-known examples from Lydney in Gloucestershire—where a man who has lost a ring devotes half its value to the god Nodens, praying that the thief may have no health until he brings it to the temple—and the tablet found in the hot spring at Bath in 1880, which asks that whoever carried off a woman named Vilbia may become as liquid as water. The Harlow Rigg tablets were written in the usual cramped, cursive Latin of the third century, full of rustic spellings and grammatical slips. One complained of the theft of a bronze cooking-pan; another of two fleeces taken from a shed; a third of a pair of boots and six silver denarii. In each case, the writer formally made over the stolen property to the presiding deity of the spring, begging that the thief might have no peace of body until he brought the thing back to the water.

There was, however, one peculiarity common to all ten tablets which I had never seen before, and which I found myself unable to account for. In every single case, the deity was addressed as *Domina Aquae*—the Lady of the Water—followed by a proper name; and in every single case, that proper name had been deliberately obliterated. It was not the work of corrosion, nor of any later Christian zealot; the letters had been beaten out with a blunt punch or the head of a nail, leaving a row of small, regular dents across the metal, and this had been done before the tablets were rolled and pierced. Whoever wrote them had felt obliged to set down the name in order to make the formula valid, and had then been afraid to leave it legible.

I had laid the ten sheets in a row upon the blotting-paper, and was just turning my attention to the eleventh—the folded tablet without a nail—when the dinner-gong sounded in the hall. I washed my hands, put on my evening coat, and went down.

During dinner Mr Forster was in high spirits. He had been out to look at a young horse in the afternoon, and he talked a great deal about the price of oats and the incompetence of the County Council in the matter of road-mending. Miss Forster sat at the foot of the table, cutting her mutton into very small squares and eating them with an air of patient endurance. It was not until the port had been set down and Miss Forster had withdrawn—having first folded her napkin into its accustomed strip—that Forster asked me how I had got on with the lead.

I gave him a brief account of the ten tablets, omitting nothing except my own puzzlement over the punched-out names, which I thought he would dismiss as a scholar's fancy. He listened with his elbows on the table, nodding now and then.

'Petty larceny,' he said, when I had finished. 'Just like to-day. A man loses his boots and wants the police to lay the thief by the heels, only in those days he dropped a bit of lead down a well instead of going to the magistrate. And what about the eleventh? The big one?'

'I have unrolled it,' I said, 'and cleaned the face, though I have not yet made a fair copy of the text. It is written in a much better hand than the others—almost a monumental script—and the Latin is tolerably correct.'

'Fetch it down,' Forster said, pushing the decanter toward me. 'I'd like to hear what it says. Let's have it in the Latin first, just to hear the ring of it, and then you can put it into English.'

I am not, as a rule, fond of performing my work as an after-dinner entertainment, but Forster was my host, and he had paid for the excavation; so I went up to the library, brought down the eleventh tablet on a sheet of cardboard, and laid it beside my glass under the chandelier. The dining-room was warm, and the heavy red curtains were drawn across the windows.

I adjusted my spectacles, tilted the lead slightly to catch the lamplight, and read it aloud. The text was short—no more than seven lines—and the letters were cut deeply with a sharp stylus:

*Quod ex hoc loco sublatum est, huic loco debetur. Quisquis habet, sive ipse abstulit sive ab alio accepit, aqua ei non sit aqua, donec huc rettulerit. Signavit qui rettulit.*

My voice sounded oddly dry in the quiet room. I paused for a moment, and then translated it for him as literally as I could.

'"What has been taken from this place is owed to this place. Whoever has it, whether he took it himself or received it from another, let water be no water to him until he bring it back hither. Signed by him who brought it back."'

Forster leaned back in his chair and let out a long breath. 'Well,' he said, 'that's a different tune altogether. No boots or cooking-pans there. Some fellow robbed the well—fished out the coins, I suppose—and got frightened and put them back.'

'It would appear so,' I said. 'And notice that there is no nail-hole. He did not need to bind the curse; he was merely recording the terms of it, or perhaps fulfilling a condition of his own release. There is no name of the deity, either—not even a punched-out one. Only *huic loco*—to this place.'

'A neat piece of work,' Forster said, reaching for his port glass. He tilted it to his lips, and then stopped, frowning. The glass was empty. 'Hullo,' he said, 'I don't remember finishing that.'

I looked down at my own glass. It, too, was empty, though I could have sworn I had barely touched it since returning from the library; and what was more, my mouth felt as though I had been chewing chalk. I reached for the water-carafe that stood between us, poured myself half a tumbler, and drank it off. It was cool enough, yet it seemed to pass down my throat without wetting it. Forster watched me, and then poured himself a full glass from the same carafe.

'The salt in that mutton,' he said, wiping his moustache with his napkin. 'I've told Hannah twice about the brine. She's getting careless.'

We sat for another half-hour talking of the Newcastle museum, to which Forster intended to present the entire collection as soon as I had finished my notes on the tablets; and I noticed that before we rose to join Miss Forster in the drawing-room, the water-carafe was completely empty.

I went to bed at eleven o'clock. My room was at the back of the house, looking out toward the dark swell of the fell where the mile-castle lay. I was tired, and fell asleep almost at once; yet I cannot have slept for more than two hours before I woke with a sensation so sharp and unpleasant that I sat upright in the dark, gasping for air.

My tongue felt swollen and rough against the roof of my mouth, like a piece of dry leather. My throat was contracted, and there was a dull, tight ache behind my eyes, such as one gets after walking all day in a high wind without a drink. I lit the candle on the night-table, poured a glass of water from the ewer on the washstand, and drank it eagerly.

I do not know how to describe what followed without sounding absurd, yet I must set it down plainly. The water was cold; I felt the weight of it against my teeth and the chill of it going down my gullet into my stomach; and yet it gave me no relief whatever. It might as well have been fine sand, or cold air. Before I had even set the tumbler down, my mouth was as parched as it had been before I touched it.

I stood there in my nightshirt, staring at the ewer, and then poured another glass and drank it slowly, holding each mouthful for a few seconds before swallowing. The result was precisely the same. My stomach felt heavy and cold with the water I had drunk, yet my throat burned as if I had been swallowing dust. I told myself that I had taken a chill on the fell, or that Forster's claret had disagreed with me, and I went back to bed; but sleep was out of the question. Every five minutes I was up again, drinking from the ewer until I had emptied it, and then rinsing my mouth with the tepid water from the shaving-jug.

By four o'clock in the morning, when the first gray light began to show round the edges of the blind, I was in a state of distress that I am ashamed to recall. A steady, thin rain had begun to fall outside. I drew up the blind, threw open the sash-window, and leaned out into the cold air, holding my cupped hands under the dripping eaves. The rain was icy on my wrists; I lapped it from my palms like a dog, and it did nothing. It ran down my chin and soaked the front of my nightshirt, and my throat remained as dry as a bone.

I dressed myself as soon as it was light enough to see—my hands were shaking so much that I could scarcely fasten my collar—and crept down the stairs without waking the house. I had no clear plan in my head, except a vague, animal notion that the air outside might ease me, and that I ought to walk down to the burn. Yet when I unbolted the side door and stepped out onto the gravel, my feet turned of their own accord up the cart-track toward the mile-castle.

The rain had stopped, leaving the grass drenched and the low sky the colour of wet slate. When I reached the south gate of the mile-castle, I saw Dodd already there, sitting on his upturned barrow beside the tool-shed, smoking a short clay pipe. He did not seem surprised to see me at half-past five in the morning. He took the pipe out of his mouth, looked at my face, and stood up.

'You've got it, sir,' he said.

I tried to answer him, but my voice came out as a hoarse croak. I cleared my throat and managed to say, 'Water.'

Dodd reached into his trousers pocket, pulled out a worn copper penny, and held it out to me on his broad, gray palm. 'Drop this in,' he said, nodding toward the well. 'It'll ease you for the day.'

I stood looking at the penny, and then at the dark circle of the well-head six yards away. Even in the miserable state I was in, the scholar's habit of mind did not wholly desert me. I remembered the eleventh tablet—the exact words I had read aloud under the chandelier the night before. *Sive ipse abstulit sive ab alio accepit.* Whether he took it himself, or received it from another. It did not ask for a penny. It did not ask for payment at all.

I put my hand into my fob pocket, where the silver denarius of Antoninus Pius still lay—the keepsake Forster had pressed upon me in the shed. I drew it out, walked past Dodd to the coping of the well, and dropped it into the shaft.

I leaned over, waiting for the small splash. None came. The dark surface of the water did not ripple, and I heard no sound of the coin striking stone or liquid. Yet at the very instant the silver left my fingers, the tightness in my throat vanished. Moisture flooded my mouth; the ache behind my eyes lifted like a shutter being drawn back; and I drew a deep, sweet breath of the damp morning air.

I sat down on the wet flagstones of the coping, feeling suddenly weak in the knees, and wiped my forehead with my handkerchief. Dodd came over and stood beside me, putting his penny back into his pocket.

'That was one of the master's,' he said quietly.

'He gave it to me yesterday,' I replied. My voice was my own again. 'Dodd, tell me plainly what happened here last month.'

He looked down into the well, and then out across the gray valley. 'It was the men that worked the bucket,' he said. 'Whoever handled the things that came up—the coins, or the pins, or the bits of lead—by the next morning his mouth was like an oven. You could drink a gallon of tea, or a quart of beer at the *Twice Brewed*, and it went through you like nothing. Old Robson was the first to try the penny. He'd heard his grandfather talk of the well at Carrawburgh, how folk used to drop a bit of silver in for luck. He dropped a penny in one morning before he started, and he was right as rain till the next day. So we all did it. A penny a day, every man that touched the stuff, and it kept it off. Till the master had me fish them out and put them in his jar.'

'And you?' I asked. 'Why did you stay?'

'I never touched the finds after that,' Dodd said. 'I only used the spade on the turf. And I put my penny in every morning all the same, when he's not looking. He doesn't know.'

I stood up and looked back down the track toward Harlow Rigg, where a thin plume of smoke was beginning to rise from the kitchen chimney. 'Mr Forster has the rest of the collection in the shed,' I said. 'And the tablets are in his library.'

'Aye,' Dodd said, and knocked the ash out of his pipe against his heel. 'He'll not put them back.'

He was right. I found Forster in the dining-room at eight o'clock, eating fried bacon and drinking cup after cup of strong tea. He looked heavy-eyed and irritable, and there was a dry, flaky whiteness at the corners of his lips. When I asked him how he had slept, he snapped that he had slept poorly, and that the mutton had been salted enough to pickle a ship's crew.

I waited until Miss Forster had left the room, and then I told him—as calmly and plainly as I could—what had happened to me in the night, what I had done at the well, and what Dodd had told me. I did not attempt to explain it; I had no explanation to offer. I simply urged him, with all the earnestness I could command, to have the whole collection—coins, altars, brooches, and tablets—returned to the well that very morning, and to leave the place alone.

I have seldom seen a man grow so angry so quickly. His face darkened to a dull plum colour, and he brought his fist down on the tablecloth so hard that the spoons jumped.

'Put them back?' he shouted. 'Are you out of your wits, sir? I've spent two summers and upwards of three hundred pounds clearing that hole! I've paid for every spadeful of earth that came out of it, and I owe no man—and no Roman bogey either—a single farthing! If you've got a touch of the ague from standing about in the damp, take a dose of quinine and don't come to my table with quarrymen's tales.'

'Mr Forster,' I said, 'you drank three cups of tea while I was speaking, and your mouth is dry now.'

He opened his lips to swear at me, stopped, and reached involuntarily for his teacup, which was empty. For a single second I saw something like doubt cross his eyes; then his stubbornness closed over it again.

'The collection goes to Newcastle on Monday,' he said, rising from the table. 'I've written to the Secretary already. If you've finished with the tablets, I'll thank you for your notes before you leave; and if you haven't, I dare say there's some one at Durham who can read them without losing his nerve.'

I packed my bag that morning and caught the afternoon train from Hexham. I left my fair copies of the eleven tablets, together with my English translations, in a sealed envelope on the hall table; it seemed the only civil thing to do, though I have since had ample reason to wish I had thrown them into the Tyne on my way to the station.

For six weeks I heard nothing from Harlow Rigg, and I returned to my own quiet routine in Cambridge, trying to persuade myself that the whole business had been an attack of fever brought on by the northern damp. Then, in the first week of October, I received a letter written in a neat, cramped hand on heavy mourning-bordered paper—though it was not mourning paper, I saw on looking closer, but merely very old stationery edged with a faint gray line. It was from Miss Forster.

She wrote with the same dry brevity that marked her conversation. Her brother, she said, was not well. He had been drinking water and weak spirits all day long for the past month, to a degree that alarmed the servants, though it seemed to give him no comfort. Dr Armstrong from Hexham had been called in, and had tested him carefully for diabetes, as was natural in such a case, but had found no trace of the disease. He had prescribed a tonic and advised a change of air, which Thomas had refused. She added, as a postscript, that the Roman things had been sent to Newcastle in September, as arranged.

I wrote back at once, begging her to send for me if I could be of any use, and suggesting—as delicately as I could—that her brother might be easier in his mind if the Newcastle Society were asked to return the collection.

Her second letter came a fortnight later. Thomas had stopped eating almost entirely, she wrote, because he could not swallow solid food; he lived on broth and milk, and even those he seemed to force down with difficulty. His temper had grown very strange. Two days earlier he had written a pressing letter to the Secretary of the museum at Newcastle, asking that the Harlow Rigg collection be lent back to him for a few weeks on the pretext of having it photographed. The Secretary had replied politely that the Council had no power to authorise a loan without a formal vote, and that the next meeting of the Council was not until the second Wednesday in November. 'He sits in the library all evening,' Miss Forster ended, 'looking at the empty table where you worked. He will not let me light the fire, because he says the heat dries the air.'

I was preparing to travel north myself—though what I could have done when I got there, I do not know—when the third letter arrived, on the morning of the third of November. It was very short.

My brother died in the night of Friday last, Miss Forster wrote. He had left his bed at some hour after midnight, in his dressing-gown and slippers, without waking any one in the house, and had walked up the track to the mile-castle. Dodd found him there at seven o'clock in the morning, lying face downward across the coping of the well, with one arm hanging down into the shaft. His leather purse lay open beside him on the grass, completely empty.

What followed in her letter was set down with the same flat, unemotional precision, as though she were reporting the loss of a cow. The well, which had stood eleven feet deep in water all through that wet summer and autumn, was entirely dry when Dodd found him. At the very bottom, lying scattered on the dark mud among the white quartz pebbles that Forster had left unmoved, were forty-one gold sovereigns. By noon, when the police-constable and Dr Armstrong came up from Hexham to view the place, the water had risen again to its usual level, and the sovereigns were out of sight. Dr Armstrong had given the cause of death on the certificate as syncope, or failure of the heart, following an obscure wasting illness; 'but he told me privately in the hall,' Miss Forster wrote, 'that if he had found a man in that state in the middle of the Sahara desert, he would have called it death from thirst.'

There was one final paragraph, which I read twice before the full meaning of it came home to me. 'I have emptied the glass jar of pennies back into the well,' she wrote, 'as Dodd seemed to think it proper, and yesterday I had the estate masons tip four cartloads of rubble and clay into the shaft and flag it over level with the ground. I do not wish to see it again.'

I sat for a long time at my desk with her letter in my hand. Poor woman—she had done what any sensible, practical lady of Northumberland would do with a dangerous pit on her land. She had closed it up for good. Yet as I thought of the eleven lines of Latin I had read aloud in that warm dining-room, a coldness settled in my stomach that had nothing to do with the November fog outside my window.

The tablet had been quite explicit. *Quod ex hoc loco sublatum est, huic loco debetur.* What has been taken from this place is owed to this place. Not to a museum in Newcastle; not to a glass case in an upper gallery; and not to be compounded for with forty-one sovereigns of King Edward's gold. It was owed at one spot on the fellside, beneath the eleven-foot bend in Hadrian's Wall; and that spot is now buried under four cartloads of rubble and a layer of sandstone flags.

I have thought a great deal, during the past year, about how the thing reached Forster and myself. The workmen who handled the coins in the mud were touched at once, and bought themselves off, day by day, with their pennies. Yet Forster had handled the finds for two summers without taking any harm, and I had carried his silver denarius in my pocket for twenty-four hours without feeling so much as a dry lip. It did not touch either of us until after dinner on that second evening, when I laid the eleventh tablet under the chandelier and read the Latin aloud—and read, in particular, that careful, legalistic clause about the man who *ab alio accepit*, who received it from another. The ancients knew very well that an inscription is not merely a record; it is an instrument, and it sleeps until a human voice gives it breath. Forster had dug up the lead, but it was I who spoke the words. I have not read an inscription aloud since that night, even in the privacy of my own rooms, and I do not intend to do so again.

As for the Harlow Rigg collection, it remains on view in the upper gallery of the museum at Newcastle, where any member of the public may inspect it on payment of sixpence. The coins are arranged in neat rows on green baize; the three uninscribed altars stand in a corner; and the eleven lead tablets are mounted under glass, each with a little white card in my own handwriting giving the Latin text and the English translation. I went up to see them once, in the spring of this year, before I wrote my letter to the Council. The room was quiet and well-lit, and the elderly attendant who sits by the door seemed glad of a little company. I asked him if he found the gallery comfortable, and whether the heating-pipes gave him any trouble. He said no, it was a pleasant enough post, and he had nothing to complain of—except that he found the room uncommonly dusty, and was always having to step down to the tap in the yard for a glass of water.
