# Brief: "The Well at the Mile-Castle"

## The job

Write one short ghost story, complete, of 3,800 to 4,300 words. It must
pass as a real story printed in a British magazine in 1903. Give back
the story only: title, byline, text. No notes before or after, except
the short list asked for at the very end of this brief.

## The project

This is one story in a collection. Each story is written in the voice
of an invented, obscure magazine writer of 1880 to 1925. The test is
whether the writers can be told apart on the page, and whether the
stories are worth reading. A story that could be handed to a different
writer without anyone noticing has failed.

## The writer

**Hester Charlton (1862–1938).** Invented. A classics lecturer at a
women's college.

- A solicitor's daughter from Hexham, beside Hadrian's Wall. At 14 her
  father took her to Carrawburgh to watch Coventina's Well being
  emptied: more than 13,000 coins and many small offerings, thrown in
  over three centuries by people who wanted something. Country people
  carried coins off by the pocketful. What she took from it: for three
  hundred years people paid the water, and then they stopped.
- She read classics at Cambridge when Cambridge gave women no degree.
  She taught Greek for 33 years. A careful, minor scholar: notes on
  Athenian funeral vases and on the lead curse tablets that Greeks and
  Romans buried in graves or dropped into wells.
- She wrote twelve stories her colleagues never knew about, signed
  "H. C."

**What a ghost is to her.** The dead are older than the gods, and they
are owed. The ancients knew the form: the offering, the day set aside,
the right words. We have kept the dead and lost the form. So a haunting
is an unpaid debt, and it is not moral. The dead do not care whether
you are good. They want what is due: water, a coin, a name said aloud.
There is a second danger that is hers alone: scholarship. To read an
inscription aloud is to do what it says.

**Her voice.**

- First person. The narrator is a scholar, and a man: she borrowed the
  authority.
- Measured, exact sentences with donnish dryness. He qualifies, then
  commits.
- Technical terms used without fuss. Measurements. One short Latin or
  Greek phrase, glossed in passing.
- Little dialogue. Educated people underplaying things.
- The ghost is felt in the body before anything else: thirst, cold
  water, a smell. If it is seen at all, it is seen as an ancient would
  describe it, not a Victorian.
- The debt is paid at a cost, or paid wrongly. The last paragraph is
  calm.
- Never: clergymen bringing comfort, Christian consolation, comic
  country people, a moral.

## This story

- **Title:** The Well at the Mile-Castle
- **Byline:** By H. C.
- **Magazine and year:** *Chambers's Journal*, Edinburgh, 1903
- **The one fixed fact:** workmen are clearing a Roman well in a
  mile-castle on Hadrian's Wall, and they keep throwing their own
  pennies into it. They will not say why.

**What this story is for.** The collection so far is delicate, eerie,
or funny. None of it is truly frightening. This is the story meant to
carry real malevolence. So:

- Somebody must be hurt, in the body, and not saved.
- Explain the history. Never explain the thing. The reader should learn
  exactly what happened and what was written, and nothing about what
  did it.
- It should not be angry. Something that only keeps accounts is worse.
- The narrator must have something at stake and must pay for it. A
  detached observer makes a story nobody is frightened by.
- Do not tidy it away at the end. Leave it loose in the world.

## Real things you may lean on

All of these are real and were known in 1903.

- Coventina's Well, Carrawburgh, emptied by John Clayton in 1876.
- A lead tablet from Lydney, Gloucestershire: a man who has lost a ring
  gives half its value to the god Nodens, and asks that the thief have
  no health until he brings it to the temple.
- A lead tablet from the hot spring at Bath, found in 1880, asking that
  whoever carried off "Vilbia" become as liquid as water.
- The Coronation of Edward VII, put off from June 1902 to 9 August.
- No well was known inside any mile-castle. A narrator of 1903 would
  find one strange.
- Scholars of the day still argued over whether Hadrian or Severus
  built the stone Wall.

You may invent the house, the people, the well, its contents and any
inscription found in it. Do not invent quotations from real texts, real
scholars or real inscriptions.

## How *Chambers's Journal* set a story in 1903

- Speech in single quotation marks, with double inside.
- "Mr", "Mrs" and "Dr" with no full point.
- "gray", never "grey". "-ise", never "-ize".
- "to-day", "to-night", "any one", "every one", "for ever".
- Long dashes with no space either side.
- The first word or two of the story in capitals.

## What the prose of the time does

- Feelings are stated outright. The author may speak to the reader.
- Stories open on someone's circumstances, not on action.
- They carry slack: a digression, a foolish opinion, a detail that
  never pays off.
- Sentences run long for this writer. Aim for a middle sentence of
  about 19 or 20 words, long paragraphs, and seven or eight semicolons
  to every thousand words. For comparison, M. R. James's "Count Magnus"
  (1904) has a middle sentence of 20 words.

## Habits to keep out

These read as modern, or as machine-made. Each one is allowed only if
you could say what it is doing there.

- A one-line paragraph that lands a point and stops.
- Aphorisms, as a habit. One is the limit.
- Lists of three.
- "Not this, but that", where the contrast is empty.
- A short sentence put first for punch, where the plain order would do.
- A last clause that sounds weighty and adds nothing. Test: what does
  it tell the reader that they did not already have?
- A balanced sentence whose second half lands on an empty word.
- A sentence that announces what is about to be said.
- A plain thing said in a tortuous way.
- A narrator telling the reader that a line is fine or cannot be
  bettered.
- A comparison that breaks when you ask what else it implies.
- Every detail paying off. Let some do nothing.
- Unbroken good taste. Let someone be foolish, or undignified.
- Any phrase that could not have been written before 1903.

Two checks before you finish. Is every sentence true to the rest of the
story? And if a sentence signals that something matters, is the thing
that matters actually in it?

## At the very end

After the story, on a separate line, list in a few words any historical
fact you used and were not sure of.

## Optional: the plot of the existing version

*Delete everything below this line to test invention as well as prose.
Keep it to compare prose on the same story.*

A scholar who reads Roman curse tablets tells it, a year on. He wrote
to a society of antiquaries advising that a collection in its museum be
put back in the ground, was politely refused, and now gives his
reasons, "as evidence of nothing".

1. In August 1902 he is asked north by Mr Forster of Harlow Rigg, a
   coalowner's son of fifty, rich, self-taught and sure of himself, who
   lives with a silent older sister. Forster has spent two summers
   clearing a mile-castle on his land.
2. The mile-castle has a well, which none should. The Wall bends eleven
   feet out of its line to take the well inside. The narrator thinks
   the well was there first.
3. Out of it have come 1,306 coins, three small altars with no
   lettering, some pins and brooches, eleven rolled lead tablets, and
   at the very bottom a thick bed of white quartz pebbles carried from
   a burn half a mile off.
4. The foreman, Dodd, a silent ex-quarryman, has reported that every
   man drops a penny in the well each morning. Asked why, he says only
   that it is a dry job. Forster had the pennies fished out and keeps
   them in a labelled jar as a joke. The men have left, all but Dodd.
5. Ten tablets are ordinary: someone has lost a pan, or two fleeces,
   and makes the lost thing over to "the Lady of the water" until the
   thief brings it there. In every one her name has been beaten out
   with a punch, by the people who wrote it.
6. The eleventh lay uppermost and has no nail. After dinner Forster
   asks him to read it aloud in Latin. It says that what is taken from
   this place is owed to this place; whoever has it, whether he took it
   or had it of another, let water be no water to him until he bring it
   again. It is signed by a man "who brought it again". Both men find
   they have emptied their glasses without noticing.
7. That night the narrator cannot quench his thirst. Water goes down
   and does nothing. By dawn he is at the window catching rain in his
   hands.
8. At the well Dodd offers him a penny. He realises payment is not what
   the tablet asks for, and drops in the one coin Forster gave him as a
   keepsake. He does not hear it reach the water. His thirst is gone at
   once. Dodd explains: whoever handled the finds went dry, and a penny
   a day eased it, until the master took the pennies.
9. Forster is angry and will not put anything back. He has paid for
   every spadeful and owes no man. The collection goes to the museum at
   Newcastle. The narrator goes home.
10. Three letters from the sister. Forster drinks all day; the doctor
    tests for diabetes and finds none. He stops eating. He asks the
    museum to lend the collection back and is told the council does not
    meet until November.
11. He is found dead at the well-head in his dressing-gown, purse
    empty. The well, eleven feet deep in water all summer, is dry:
    forty-one sovereigns lie on the mud. By noon the water is back. The
    doctor says privately that anywhere else he would call it death
    from thirst.
12. The sister empties the pennies back and has the well filled in. The
    narrator sees too late what that means: the collection was owed at
    one place only, and that place is gone.
13. He believes it reached Forster and himself only when the clause
    about one who "had it of another" was said aloud, and it was he who
    said it. He no longer reads inscriptions aloud.
14. The collection is on show in the museum, each tablet with a card in
    his handwriting giving the reading and a translation. The attendant
    in that room complains of nothing, except that he finds it dusty.
