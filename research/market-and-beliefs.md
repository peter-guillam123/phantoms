## The British Library series

- "Tales of the Weird" began in late 2018. Each volume has its own
  editor, most often Mike Ashley; there are now more than 70. (extract)
  [Complete list](https://medium.com/flexible-head/the-british-library-tales-of-the-weird-the-complete-list-c413070445d4)
- The volume closest to this project is no. 3, "Glimpses of the Unknown:
  Lost Ghost Stories" (ed. Ashley, 2018): 18 stories from the 1890s to
  1929, none reprinted since first publication. Reviewers say some of its
  authors "disappeared without leaving any other trace". Of one, Jack
  Edwards, Ashley says he could find nothing at all. (extract)
  [Vault of Evil](https://vaultofevil.wordpress.com/2019/05/21/mike-ashley-ed-glimpses-of-the-unknown-lost-ghost-stories/),
  [Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/41219437-glimpses-of-the-unknown)
- Its contents: Hugh E. Wright, "On the Embankment" (The Blue Magazine,
  1919); Elsie Norris, "The Mystery of the Gables" (1908); Austin
  Philips, "The Missing Word"; Huan Mee, "Phantom Death"; Firth Scott,
  "The Wraith of the Rapier" (1911); James Barr, "The Soul of Maddalina
  Tonelli" (1909); Jack Edwards, "Haunted!"; Percy James Brebner, "Our
  Strange Traveller"; Guy Thorne, "A Regent of Love Rhymes"; Francis
  Xavier, "Amid the Trees" (1911); Mary Schultze, "The River's Edge"
  (1912); Mary Reynolds, "A Futile Ghost"; Lumley Deakin, "Ghosts";
  Elizabeth Jordan, "Kearney"; Philippa Forest, "When Spirits Steal";
  Eric Purves, "The House of the Black Evil" (Pearson's, 1929);
  E. F. Benson, "The Woman in the Veil" (1928); F. Britten Austin, "The
  Treasure of the Tombs" (1921). (extract)
- Who such people were, where known: "Huan Mee" was two brothers,
  Charles and Walter Mansfield, sharing a pen name (extract). Percy
  Brebner worked for the Stock Exchange; Austin Philips was a Post Office
  official; F. Britten Austin a Stock Exchange clerk and then an army
  officer; Hugh E. Wright an actor; James Barr and Firth Scott
  journalists (all memory). So: clerks, officials, pressmen and an actor,
  writing on the side. That fits the ten.
- Related volumes: "Queens of the Abyss" (Ashley, 2020), forgotten women
  of the early pulps; "The Platform Edge" (2019), railways; "The Night
  Wire" (Aaron Worth), haunted telephones, telegraphs and wireless;
  "Cornish Horrors" (Joan Passey, 2021); "Celtic Weird" (Johnny Mains);
  "Chill Tidings" and "Haunters at the Hearth" (Tanya Kirk), Christmas
  stories; "Holy Ghosts", church stories. (extract for titles and
  editors, memory for some years)

## The magazines

Three generations, roughly.

**The shilling monthlies (1860s to about 1905).** Read by the
drawing-room; took quiet, well-bred ghosts.

- Temple Bar, 1860–1906, about a shilling; often ran ghost stories.
  (read)
- The Cornhill, 1860–1975. (read) Sixpence from 1883, a shilling again
  from 1896. (extract)
- Belgravia, 1866–99, edited at first by M. E. Braddon. (extract)
- The Argosy, edited by Mrs Henry Wood. (extract) 1865–1901. (memory)
- Longman's, November 1882 to October 1905; Andrew Lang's column. (read)
- Blackwood's, Edinburgh, 1817–1980, Tory, known as "Maga"; printed
  much of its matter unsigned into the 1900s; a favourite of soldiers and
  colonial officials. (read for dates; extract for anonymity)
- Chambers's Journal, 1832–1956, a cheap weekly for family reading.
  (read)

**The sixpenny illustrated monthlies (1891 on).** Read on trains; wanted
plot, pace and a picture on every other page.

- The Strand, January 1891 to March 1950; edited by H. Greenhough Smith
  to 1930; sold nearly 500,000 a month. (read)
- The Idler, 1892, Jerome K. Jerome and Robert Barr. (extract)
- The Windsor, January 1895 to September 1939. (read)
- Pearson's, 1896 to November 1939. (read)
- The Royal, November 1898; Harmsworth's (later The London Magazine),
  July 1898, threepence. (extract)

**The all-fiction magazines (1905 on).** Cheap paper, no pictures, a
great many stories.

- The Novel Magazine, April 1905, called the first British all-fiction
  pulp. (extract)
- The Story-Teller (1907), The Red Magazine (1908). (memory)
- Hutchinson's Mystery-Story Magazine, running by 1923, merged away in
  October 1927; it reprinted a good deal from the American Weird Tales.
  (extract)
- 20-Story Magazine, Odhams, from 1922 or 1923 to 1940. (extract)

**Also:** the Christmas number, where the ghost story had lived since
Dickens (extract); newspaper syndication through Tillotson's Fiction
Bureau of Bolton, 1873–1935, which sold stories to provincial papers
(extract); Lady Cynthia Asquith's "The Ghost Book" (1926) and Christine
Campbell Thomson's "Not at Night" (from 1925), the anthologies that
closed the period (extract).

## How an unknown got printed

- Pay, by Arnold Bennett's "How to Become an Author" (1903): an unknown
  got roughly half a guinea to three guineas per thousand words; a
  leading magazine paid about fifteen guineas for a 5,000-word story.
  The search summaries of this book did not agree exactly, and the text
  should be read. (extract)
  [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/cu31924027103559)
- For scale, the Strand paid Conan Doyle about £36 a story for the first
  Sherlock Holmes tales in 1891. (extract)
- Usual length, 2,000 to 6,000 words. (memory)
- Many pieces were unsigned or signed with initials, above all in
  Blackwood's. Women often used initials or a man's name. Real cases:
  "Vernon Lee" (Violet Paget); "G. G. Pendarves" (Gladys Gordon Trenery,
  1885–1938, a Cornishwoman who sold 19 stories to Weird Tales and of
  whom almost nothing else is known). (extract)
- Help for beginners arrived during the period: A. P. Watt, the first
  literary agent, from about 1880; the Society of Authors, 1884 (memory);
  the first "Writers' and Artists' Year-Book", for 1906, a shilling list
  of papers and what they wanted. (extract)

## What people thought a ghost was

Each of these is a position one of the ten could hold or argue with.

- **A hallucination with a cause.** The Society for Psychical Research
  (founded 1882) asked 17,000 people whether they had ever seen or heard
  something that was not there. About one in ten said yes. The report
  (1894) argued that apparitions of people at the moment of their death
  were too common to be chance. (extract)
  [SPR encyclopedia](https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/ghosts-and-apparitions-psi-research-overview/)
- **A recording.** Charles Babbage argued in 1837 that every word spoken
  leaves a permanent trace in the air: "The air itself is one vast
  library." In the 1880s Eleanor Sidgwick and Edmund Gurney of the SPR
  suggested that buildings might hold records of events, replayed to
  certain people. (read for the outline; her exact words not checked)
  [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stone_Tape_theory)
  This is Trembath's ground.
- **A true story from the public.** W. T. Stead filled the Christmas
  1891 number of his Review of Reviews with readers' own "Real Ghost
  Stories". (extract) Staniforth would have seen it as proof of the
  market.
- **A thing in the mind.** Vernon Lee, in the preface to "Hauntings"
  (1890), said her ghosts were "spurious ghosts (according to me the only
  genuine ones)", which "haunted certain brains". (extract) Tugwell and
  Gadd are both near this.
- **The dead, wanting to talk.** Spiritualism grew fast after the war.
  Oliver Lodge's "Raymond" (1916), about his dead son, went through a
  dozen printings by 1919. Societies affiliated to the national union
  are said to have risen from 145 in 1914 to 309 in 1919. (extract, one
  source for the figures)
- **A fraud.** Ada Deane photographed "spirit faces" above the Cenotaph
  crowd during the silence each 11 November from 1922. In 1924 the Daily
  Sketch showed the faces were those of living footballers and boxers.
  (extract)
- **Something pretending to be the dead.** The 1920 Lambeth Conference
  warned that spiritualism meant "the subordination of the intelligence
  and the will to unknown forces or personalities". (extract) The
  Catholic line, as in R. H. Benson's novel "The Necromancers" (1909),
  was blunter: what answers at a séance is not your son. (memory) None
  of the ten holds this view outright. It is a gap worth knowing about.
- **A craft.** M. R. James thought a ghost story needed a familiar
  setting, a slow build ("the nicely managed crescendo") and a ghost that
  meant harm. (extract for the phrase; memory for the rest) His first
  two stories ran in the National Review and the Pall Mall Magazine in
  1895.
