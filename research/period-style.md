# How the stories of the time were actually written

Chris asked whether the ten voices are informed by the styles of the
period, and whether more research was needed to make the stories
believable as historical objects. The plain answer was: partly, and yes.
This file records what was then checked, and what is still to do.

## Where the first voice rules fell short

The voice rules in `personas/` were written from general knowledge of
period fiction, not from reading it in this project. Three things pulled
them towards the present day:

1. **The house style leaked in.** The specimens were written under
   Chris's rules for modern prose: short words, short sentences, no em
   dashes. Period magazine prose is built on the semicolon and the dash.
2. **Modern taste in ghost stories.** The rules favour restraint, an
   unseen ghost and an ending with no explanation. That is what is admired
   now. The ordinary magazine story of 1900 explained itself, tidied up,
   and told you what became of everyone.
3. **Nothing was checked against a source.** Dialect, forms of address and
   magazine conventions were all from memory.

## What was read

The Internet Archive has scanned individual issues of the magazines, with
searchable text. Read for this pass, on 11 November 2026:

- Twelve weekly issues of *Chambers's Journal*, October 1899 to April
  1900 (Archive identifiers beginning `sim_chambers-journal_`).
- Three monthly issues of *Temple Bar*, December 1890, January 1891 and
  December 1891 (`sim_temple-bar_`).
- An 1870s text of Jane Austen's *Persuasion*, to check every line the
  first story quotes.

## What they show

**Chambers's house style, 1899–1900.** Counted across the twelve issues:

- "Mr", "Mrs" and "Dr" never take a full point (221 to 0).
- Speech is in single quotation marks, with double inside.
- "gray", never "grey" (36 to 0). "-ise", never "-ize".
- "to-day", "to-night", "any one", "every one", "for ever", all as two
  words or hyphened. "drawing-room", "arm-chair". But "bedroom",
  "upstairs", "downstairs", "bookcase", "firelight" as one word.
- Dashes are long and unspaced.
- Short stories are signed ("By R. Ramsay"), run 3,200 to 4,300 words,
  and are usually complete in one number. Longer ones run over two to
  four weeks with "IN FOUR CHAPTERS.—CHAPTER II." under the title.
- The first word or two is set in capitals after a large initial.

*Temple Bar*, by contrast, uses double quotation marks and "Mrs." with a
point. So house style belongs to the magazine, not the writer. The same
author looks different in each.

**The prose, measured.** Four real stories, counted by a short script:

| Story | Words | Words per sentence (mean / middle) | Semicolons per 1,000 words | Dashes per 1,000 | Exclamation marks per 1,000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| "A Just Sentence", Chambers's, Feb 1900 | 3,202 | 17 / 13 | 8 | 8 | 1 |
| "The Chaperon", Chambers's, Jan 1900 | 4,286 | 17 / 14 | 13 | 11 | 8 |
| "A New Sensation", Temple Bar, Dec 1891 | 6,179 | 23 / 18 | 12 | 7 | 8 |
| *"The Reader", ours* | 3,660 | 18 / 13 | 15 | 5 | 0 |

Two surprises. Popular magazine sentences were not especially long: the
middle sentence in Chambers's is 13 or 14 words. What makes the prose
feel old is the punctuation, about one semicolon and one dash to every
hundred words, and the habit of stating feelings outright. And the
stories are heavily plotted: a convict and the barrister who convicted
him; jewels in the wrong bag. They open on scenery or on social
placement, and they end with everything settled.

**Ghost stories in Chambers's.** The nine issues from December 1899 and
January 1900 contain no ghost story at all. That is a small sample, and
the Journal's separate Christmas numbers were not found, but it was
enough to withdraw a claim on the editor's page that the Journal printed
"a good many". The page now says Ormiston got them in when he could.

## What this changed

- Stories and the extracts quoted from them no longer follow Chris's
  house style at all. They follow the writer and the magazine.
- Each story is set in the house style of the magazine it is said to
  have appeared in, and the page says so.
- The first story was checked against the table above before it was
  shown to anyone. It is lighter on dashes and has no exclamation marks,
  both of which are in character for Tugwell, and it was written with the
  period's habits in mind: it opens on the heroine's circumstances, lets a
  servant tell the history, and has a closing paragraph saying what
  became of everybody.

## Still to do before each of the other nine writes

The same reading, for that writer's own magazines. In order of need:

1. **Kenworthy.** A real dialect sketch from the *Cotton Factory Times*
   or *Ben Brierley's Journal*. His spelling of the dialect is from
   memory and is the least safe thing in the project.
2. **Protheroe.** Her spare, Bible-cadenced voice may be thirty years
   ahead of its time. It is closer to Caradoc Evans (1915) than to
   anything I know from the 1890s. Read *Wales* and Allen Raine, then
   either date her style later or soften it.
3. **Gadd.** An evening paper short story and an issue of *Hutchinson's
   Mystery-Story Magazine* from the 1920s. Not found on the Archive yet.
4. **Tobin.** *The Irish Monthly* and a Rosa Mulholland story.
5. **Dalgleish, Lovegrove, Trembath, Charlton.** *Blackwood's* and *The
   Cornhill*, both of which the Archive appears to have.
6. **Staniforth.** *Pearson's* and *The Strand*, 1897–1902.

Two limits. The Archive's text is machine-read and full of small errors,
so it is good for style and poor for exact quotation. And I have read
openings, endings and counts, not whole runs of each magazine.
