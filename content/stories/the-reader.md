MISS LYLE was nine-and-twenty when her father died, and she learned in the same fortnight that a vicarage belongs to the vicar. The new incumbent was a civil young man with a wife and a perambulator; he begged that she would not hurry herself, and came twice with a foot-rule to measure the study. She did not hurry herself. She sold the piano, gave the cat to the sexton, and answered an advertisement.

The advertisement desired a lady, by birth and education, as companion and reader to an elderly gentlewoman in the West of England; a clear and agreeable voice was indispensable; the salary was forty pounds. Miss Lyle had read aloud to her father every evening since her mother’s death, and supposed that her voice would do. She was engaged by letter, in a hand so upright and so evenly spaced that she formed from it a tolerably exact idea of Miss Venning before she saw her.

Northover stood at the end of the High Street of Wickbury, where the pavement leaves off and the Bath road begins: a square stone house behind a square lawn, with a cedar on the lawn and wire blinds in the lower windows. It contained Mrs Venning, who was nearly eighty; Miss Venning, her daughter, who was past fifty, and looked it conscientiously; an elderly maid named Hannah, a cook, and a girl. Mrs Venning’s sight had so far failed that she could tell the window from the wall, and the lamp from the fire, and very little else; but she sat upright in a high-backed chair, in a cap of real lace, and she missed nothing that was said in a room, nor, Miss Lyle came to think, much that was not.

The duties were light. There were letters to be written in the morning, and wools to be matched, and a walk to be taken as far as the old turnpike and back; and every evening, from eight o’clock until half-past nine, there was the reading. Everything in the house led up to the reading, as everything in a cathedral town leads up to the service. Dinner was at half-past six, that it might be over; the drawing-room fire was made up at a quarter to eight, that it might not want touching; and at eight precisely Hannah brought in the lamps.

‘You will find my mother very particular,’ Miss Venning had said, on the first afternoon. ‘She has been read to for a great many years, and she knows the books. I may tell you, Miss Lyle, that we have not been fortunate in our readers.’

Miss Lyle said that she would do her best. It was the cook who told her, on the second day, that six ladies had held the situation in five years; and nobody else told her anything at all.

* * *

There are rooms in which the furniture seems to have been set down by chance, and others in which every chair is a kind of statement. The drawing-room at Northover was of the second sort. Mrs Venning’s chair stood on the right of the hearth. On the left, across the rug, stood a low chair covered in faded green rep, with a small table at its elbow, and upon the table a little lamp with a green shade; and at the round table in the middle of the room, under the large lamp, sat Miss Venning with her knitting.

Miss Lyle, on the first evening, went naturally to the low chair. It was plainly the reader’s chair—it faced the old lady; the little lamp was lit and ready. She had her hand upon the back of it when Miss Venning spoke.

‘I think you will find the light better at the table, Miss Lyle.’

The light was not better at the table, but Miss Lyle sat there; and she sat there every evening afterwards, with Miss Venning’s needles going at her elbow. Every evening Hannah lit the little lamp with the others, and set it on the little table, and trimmed it; and every evening at half-past nine she put it out again. Nobody sat by it. Miss Lyle observed that the old lady, who could tell a lamp from a fire, kept her face turned towards it while she listened; and she observed also that it was not a thing one could ask about. She had not lived nine-and-twenty years in a vicarage without learning to know such things when she met them.

They began with *Cranford*, which went very well. Mrs Venning knew it nearly by heart, and when Miss Lyle read ‘Mr Holbrook’ for ‘Mr Hoggins’ she was put right before the sentence was out; but it was done kindly, and at the end of ten days the old lady was pleased to say that Miss Lyle read like a gentlewoman, and did not gabble. Then Hannah was sent to the bookcase in the morning-room, and came back with a thick little volume in rubbed cloth, which held two of Miss Austen’s novels, and fell open of itself at the second.

‘We will have *Persuasion*,’ said Mrs Venning. ‘We have it every winter.’

Miss Venning counted her stitches.

The first three chapters were got through in two evenings. Miss Lyle had always been fond of the book, and she read it with pleasure; she was aware that she was reading well. On the third evening she came to the fourth chapter, in which, as every one knows, very little happens: the reader is merely told, in four pages, what had happened seven years before—how Anne Elliot at nineteen had engaged herself to a young sailor with nothing but himself to recommend him, and how her godmother, who had almost a mother’s love and a mother’s rights, had talked her out of it.

‘“—But Lady Russell,”’ read Miss Lyle, ‘“whom she had always loved and relied on, could not, with such steadiness of opinion, and such tenderness of manner, be continually advising her in vain. She was persuaded to believe the engagement a wrong thing: indiscreet, improper, hardly capable of success, and not deserving it. But it was not a merely selfish caution—”’

‘You have left something out, my dear,’ said Mrs Venning.

Miss Lyle begged her pardon, found the place with her finger, and read the two sentences again.

‘No,’ said the old lady. ‘Before that—where she speaks to Lady Russell.’

‘I do not think she speaks, ma’am—not in this place. It is only said that she was persuaded.’

‘She speaks,’ said Mrs Venning.

There was a silence, in which Miss Lyle became aware that the needles at her elbow had stopped. She looked down the page, and over it, and back. She was a conscientious person; she would have been glad to find herself in the wrong.

‘I am afraid—it is not here,’ she said.

‘Then we will leave off for to-night,’ said Mrs Venning; ‘and to-morrow, if you please, you will begin the chapter again.’

* * *

She began the chapter again on the morrow, and on the day after; and each evening, at the same line, the thin hand was lifted from the arm of the high chair. It was done without temper. Mrs Venning did not suggest that Miss Lyle was careless, or that her eyes were at fault; she said only, in the voice of one who can wait, that it had not been read yet, and that they would try again to-morrow. By the end of a week Miss Lyle could have said the fourth chapter of *Persuasion* in her sleep, and frequently did. By the end of a fortnight she had begun to watch the drawing-room clock from dinner-time, and to feel, as its hands came round to eight, what she had not felt since she was a child and sent for to the study.

She did what a sensible woman could. She went to the morning-room and took down the other volumes on the shelf, and turned over every leaf of them, in case a page had strayed. She borrowed *Persuasion* from the circulating library in the High Street, in a newer edition, and compared the two line by line. The words were the same in both. The only difference she could discover was in the Northover copy itself: against the place where she was nightly stopped some one had made, a long time ago and with a very fine pencil, a small cross in the margin. On the fly-leaf, in a pointed hand, was written ‘Emily Venning,’ and a date which Miss Lyle did not think it her business to subtract.

She said nothing of the cross. But she did, on the second Sunday, walking back from church beside Miss Venning, venture to ask whether the book had perhaps once been read to Mrs Venning in some different way.

‘My mother has been read to by a great many people,’ said Miss Venning. She walked on a few yards, and then added, with her eyes on the pavement, that her mother’s memory was not what it had been; that old people took fancies; and that Miss Lyle might, she thought, with perfect propriety go straight on to the fifth chapter, and see whether it were noticed.

‘I think it would be noticed,’ said Miss Lyle.

‘Yes,’ said Miss Venning. ‘It has always been noticed.’ And she began to speak of the collection.

It was the cook, again, who supplied what the drawing-room withheld. One of the six ladies, it appeared, had been clever, and kind-hearted with it; she had sat up overnight and written out a speech for the young lady in the book, very pretty and proper, and had read it off next evening, as bold as brass, in the middle of the chapter. The mistress had heard her to the end. ‘That is not it,’ the mistress had said; ‘but I am obliged to you;’ and the lady had had her month’s notice in the morning.

Miss Lyle had thought of doing the same thing. She was thankful afterwards that she had only thought of it.

It was on the sixteenth evening that Mrs Venning said the one thing which she ever said upon the subject. The chapter had been read; the hand had been lifted; Miss Lyle had closed the book. The old lady sat for some time with her face towards the little lamp.

‘There is a question in it,’ she said. ‘I was not attending. One cannot be always attending—and when I would have attended, it was not read again.’

Nobody answered her. Miss Venning rang for Hannah, and the lamps were put out.

* * *

In the last week of November Miss Lyle took cold. It was a heavy cold, of the kind that settles; by the Wednesday she was reading through a handkerchief, and by the Thursday morning she had no voice at all, but only a whisper, which is of no use to a reader. She was sent to bed, in the room over the drawing-room, with a fire, which she had not expected; and in the afternoon Hannah came up with black-currant tea, and stayed, as she had never stayed before, to see it drunk.

Hannah had been at Northover two-and-forty years. She stood at the foot of the bed with her hands folded on her apron, and she said that it was a pity about the reading, for the mistress would miss it, and Miss Charlotte had her working-party at the Vicarage of a Thursday, and never read in any case—and then, having looked at the door, she said the rest.

It was Miss Emily that had used to read. She read every evening for eleven winters, in the low chair, by the little lamp, which the mistress had bought for her on purpose, to spare her eyes; and a sweet reader she was—you would think it was people talking. Miss Emily was the younger by nine years. There had been a Mr Rowe—he was assistant to old Dr Bird, and lodged over the saddler’s, and had no family that any one ever heard of. Nothing had come of it. Nothing could come of it, without the mistress; and it was Miss Charlotte, who had seen them at the gate, that went to the mistress in the first place. After that there was no more Mr Rowe at the house, and nothing said. That was the worst of it, in Hannah’s opinion, that there was nothing said. Miss Emily was never forbidden, and so she could never ask. It was not a house where things were said.

‘And then that winter they came to this same book, miss, and to this same place in it; and I was in the room with the coals, or I should not have believed it. She read on, the same as if it was printed there, where the young lady goes to her godmother and speaks for herself. All in book words—that she was not a child; that she knew he was poor; that she would wait any time that was thought right, if only she might be told that she might wait. And would she be answered. I never heard reading like it. Miss Charlotte had her work in her lap, and never moved.’

‘And Mrs Venning?’ whispered Miss Lyle.

‘The mistress said, “Go on, my dear,”’ said Hannah. ‘So Miss Emily went on—with the book. She read to the end of the chapter, and shut it, and went up. She went to her aunt at Clifton after Christmas, for a change—and it was a chill, they said, in the autumn. She never came back to Northover. Mr Rowe went to the Colonies.’

Hannah took the cup.

‘The mistress has had the book every winter since, and I have lit the little lamp, for she likes to see it. Miss Charlotte could say the piece over, word for word; she sat where she sits now. But she’ll not read it. It is not for me to say why not. Only it would want answering, miss, wouldn’t it, if it was asked—and there’s some would as soon not hear what the answer was.’

* * *

Miss Venning went out at seven, with her goloshes and her work-bag. Miss Lyle heard the front door, and the gate; she heard Hannah go heavily through the hall to the kitchen stairs; and then she heard the house settle, as a house does when the person who manages it has left it. She lay and watched the fire. She was sorry for the old lady below, sitting at the hour of the reading with nothing to listen to; and she was more sorry, she found, for some one else, though she could not have said whether it was for the elder sister or the younger.

The drawing-room clock struck eight. It had a thin, hurried strike, which came up plainly through the floor.

She did not at first take notice of the other sound, because it was a sound to which she was so well accustomed; it was only by degrees that she understood she ought not to be hearing it. Somebody was reading aloud in the room beneath. It was not the old lady’s voice, which was deep and a little cracked, nor Hannah’s; it was a light voice, and a young one, and it went on evenly, with the rise and fall of a person who reads well and is used to it—pausing at the stops, and taking up again. She could not distinguish a word. But she had read that chapter night after night for five weeks, and she knew the length of every sentence in it as one knows the turns of a stair; and she lay with her hands shut, and followed the voice down the page.

It came to the place. It did not stop. It went on—for how long she could never afterwards determine, though she thinks not above a minute—in a different measure: quicker, and lower, and with breaks in it that were not the breaks of print. Then it ceased.

And then, quite distinctly, Mrs Venning spoke. It was a single word, or two at most. After it the light voice began again, in the old even way, and went on to what must have been the end of the chapter; and after that there was no sound at all, except the fire.

Miss Lyle got up. She would say, if she were asked, that she had been brought up to believe that the dead are in better hands than ours, and have something else to do than to walk about Somersetshire; and she believes it now. She put on her dressing-gown and went to the head of the stairs, because some one, clearly, had come in, and she thought it right to know who.

The hall lamp was burning. The drawing-room door was shut, as it always was, and there was a light beneath it. At the same moment Hannah came through from the back with a tray.

‘Who is with Mrs Venning?’ Miss Lyle whispered over the banisters.

Hannah looked up. ‘No one, miss. She rang at eight, when I was going in to sit with her, and said I was not wanted, for she was being read to. I made sure you had come down.’

They went in together. Mrs Venning was asleep in the high chair, with her hands laid one upon the other, and her face turned towards the left of the hearth. The fire had burnt low. The large lamp was lit upon the round table, as Hannah had left it; and upon the little table, where Hannah had not lit it, there being no reading that night, the little lamp was burning steadily under its green shade. The book was not upon the round table, where Miss Lyle had put it down on the Wednesday. It lay open on the seat of the low chair.

Neither of them touched it. They were still standing there when the gate clicked, and Miss Venning’s key was heard in the door.

Miss Venning was told, in the hall, by Hannah, in six words. She stood a moment with her work-bag in her hand. Then she went into the drawing-room by herself, and shut the door after her; and what she did there, or looked at, nobody knows. She was not gone five minutes. When she came out she was drawing off her gloves.

‘You should not be out of your bed, Miss Lyle,’ she said; and then, in exactly the same tone, ‘Hannah, you may put out the little lamp.’

* * *

Mrs Venning came down to breakfast next morning, which she did not always do. She was led to her place; she felt for her cup; and she turned her face towards the foot of the table with an expression which Miss Lyle had not seen upon it before.

‘I am obliged to you, my dear,’ she said. ‘You read it very nicely last night. You did not leave it out.’

Miss Lyle had no voice. She looked at Miss Venning.

‘Miss Lyle was in bed with her cold, mamma,’ said Miss Venning.

‘Then it was good of her to get up,’ said Mrs Venning. She stirred her tea. ‘I answered it,’ she said, to nobody in particular. ‘I said yes. I ought to have said it at the time—but one cannot be always attending.’

Miss Venning had the tea-pot in her hand. She held it for a moment without pouring; and then she turned to Miss Lyle and looked at her very steadily—not as one who asks a favour, but as one who states the terms of a situation.

‘It was very good of Miss Lyle,’ she said.

Miss Lyle bowed. She could not, as it happened, have done anything else; but she has asked herself since whether, with all the voice in the world, she would have done differently, and she has not been able to feel sure of it. So it was settled at Northover who had read on the Thursday night, and it has never been unsettled. Hannah, when the subject was approached, said that the mistress had said so.

‘Do you take sugar, Miss Lyle?’ said Miss Venning.

She had poured out Miss Lyle’s tea twice a day since October.

There is not much more to tell. That evening the fifth chapter was begun; and as Miss Lyle was still unable to speak above a whisper, it was read by Miss Venning, who said that she did not read aloud, and who read it very ill, in the voice in which she read the list of subscriptions at the Dorcas meeting. Her mother did not correct her once. The little lamp was not lit, that night or afterwards; and in the spring the low chair went to the upholsterer’s to be covered.

Mrs Venning died in the April of the next year but one; and Miss Venning, in giving up the house, gave Miss Lyle an excellent character and the thick little volume from the morning-room, saying that her mother had wished it. Miss Lyle has the book still. The pencil cross is in the margin. She has an explanation of that Thursday, which is that an old lady who has a chapter by heart may very well say it over to herself in the firelight, and say, half asleep, what once came next; and it is a sensible explanation, and has served her for most purposes. It does not account for the little lamp. She reads aloud a great deal in her present situation, and is thought to do it well; but she has never read that chapter to any one, and when the book is asked for, she finds that she has mislaid it.
