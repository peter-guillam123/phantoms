IN the October of last year I wrote to the Secretary of a certain Society of Antiquaries, of which I have the honour to be a corresponding member, to advise that a collection lately presented to its museum should be taken out of its case and put back into the ground. The Secretary replied, with great civility, that the Council had considered my letter. I do not complain of that. I have sat upon councils, and I know what I should have thought of such a letter from a man whose business it is to read inscriptions, and not to bury them. But I was asked for my reasons, and I could not give them in a form that a council may minute; and so I set them down here, where nobody need act upon them, and where it will be understood that I offer them as an account of what I saw, and as evidence of nothing.

I lecture upon ancient history at a college which it is not necessary to name, and for some years I have given what leisure I have to the leaden tablets which the Greeks and Romans were accustomed to bury in graves, or to drop into wells and springs, when they wished to hand an enemy over to the powers below. It is a small subject, and not an agreeable one. The tablets are for the most part ill written and worse spelt, and they are concerned with stolen cloaks, and lawsuits, and chariot-horses, and other men’s wives. Our own island has yielded very few. There is the well-known one from Lydney, in which a man who has lost a ring makes over the half of its value to the god, and prays that the thief may have no health until he bring it to the temple; and there is one from the hot spring at Bath, which desires that the person who has carried off a certain Vilbia may be made as liquid as water. I mention these two because between them they contain the whole of what I have to tell, and I had read them both a hundred times, and had seen nothing in either but a point of grammar.

In the June of 1902 I had a letter from a Mr Forster, of Harlow Rigg, in Northumberland, of whom I had not heard before. He wrote that he had upon his property a mile-castle of the Roman Wall; that he had been clearing it out for two summers, at his own charges; that he had come upon a well in it; and that out of the well, among a great deal else, his men had taken some pieces of rolled lead with nails through them, which a neighbour, who took in the *Classical Review*, had told him were in my line. Would I come and look at them? There was a bed, and there was fishing, though he understood that scholars did not fish. I went north in the second week of August, as soon as the Coronation, which had been put off from June, was at last got over, and the trains were fit to travel in.

Mr Forster was a man of fifty, a bachelor, and the son of a Tyneside coalowner, who had left him a great deal of money and an education that had stopped at sixteen. He had bought Harlow Rigg five years before, for the shooting: eleven hundred acres of grass and stone, and a large cold house. He had then found himself the owner of two miles of the Wall; and he had taken to it as such men take to a thing, with his whole weight. He had read everything, and he had got most of it by heart, and a little of it wrong. It was his fixed opinion that the Romans in Britain were precisely the English in India, and he would point out to me the hill-station, and the cantonment, and the place where the subalterns must have played some game with a ball. I did not find that I disliked him for it. He was generous, and exact about money, and uneasy with his servants; and he lived alone there with a sister, who was older than he, and who said very little.

The Wall in that part runs along the edge of the great cliffs of whinstone that face the north, and the mile-castle stands in a dip between two of them, where a man may pass through. I need not describe it at length. It is like the rest of them: a walled yard of some twenty yards by seventeen, built against the back of the Wall, with a gate in the Wall and a gate opposite—and when Mr Forster began upon it, it was a heap of nettles with sheep in it. I am not concerned with the question whether it was Hadrian that built it, or Severus, upon which I heard a great deal at Harlow Rigg, and upon which I have no opinion that is worth the paper. But two things about it I must set down. The first is that it has a well. I do not know of another mile-castle that has one; the men who held them were few, and drew their water, as I suppose, from the nearest burn. The second is that the Wall goes out of its way to take the well in. For forty yards upon either side it leaves the line that the ground would give it, and bends southward by a matter of eleven feet, and so brings within the yard a thing which, upon any natural line, it must have left outside to the north. The engineers who laid out that Wall did nothing without a reason. I think that the well was there before them, and that they were told to enclose it; and I have wondered, since, by whom they were told, and whether it was to keep the well for the soldiers, or to keep it from somebody else.

It is in the south-west corner of the yard: a round shaft, three feet and four inches across, lined with squared stones to a depth of nine feet, and below that cut through the living rock. Mr Forster’s men had cleared it to the bottom, which is at twenty-three feet, and the water had come in again upon them and stood, when I saw it, at twelve feet from the top. It was very clear and exceedingly cold. They had brought up, in the two summers, the following things, which I give from Mr Forster’s list, for he had listed everything, and had written upon every packet the depth at which it was found. Of coins, thirteen hundred and six, nearly all of brass, and running from Trajan to the sons of Constantine. Of small altars without any lettering, three. Of pins, brooches, beads, and the like, about a hundred and forty. Of leaden tablets, eleven. And below all this, at the very bottom, where there was nothing Roman at all, a bed some two feet thick of white pebbles of quartz, every one of them about the size of a pigeon’s egg, to the number of many thousands. There is no quartz upon that hill. The nearest is in the bed of a burn, half a mile to the south. I asked Mr Forster what he made of them, and he said that they were ballast.

He told me the matter of the pennies upon my first evening, as a good story. His foreman was a man named Dodd, who had been a quarryman, and was the steadiest workman, he said, that he had ever employed; and about the middle of the first summer Dodd had come to him and said that he thought the master should know that the men were putting money down the well. Each of them, when he came upon the ground in the morning, went to the well-head before he took up his tools and dropped a penny in. There was no talk about it, and no laughing. They were not men who had pennies to spare. Mr Forster had asked the reason, and Dodd had said that it was a dry job; and beyond that he could get nothing from Dodd, or from any of them.

‘So I let it run to the end of the season,’ said Mr Forster, ‘and then I had the lot of them fished up with the rest. Four pounds eleven and sevenpence. There they are.’

They were in a glass jar upon the chimney-piece of his library, with a label in his hand: ‘Offerings to the Well, in the reigns of Victoria and of Edward the Seventh.’ He was very much pleased with the label. He added that the men had not liked it, and that when he opened the work again in the spring the half of them had not come back; and that those who came had begun again with their pennies upon the first morning, and he had forbidden it, as a thing that would get the excavation laughed at; and that in the week before I came the last of them had left him, except Dodd.

I worked upon the tablets for three days, at a table in the library window. They had been rolled, each upon itself, and pierced with an iron nail, of which in most cases only a stain of rust was left; and they had to be opened by a quarter of an inch at a time, and the writing brought up with a soft brush, and read by a candle held low at the side. Ten of them were what I had expected. A man named Docilis had lost a bronze pan. A woman, whose name I could not recover, had lost two fleeces and a hood. Somebody had lost the price of a pig. In each case the loser made the missing thing over to the Lady of the water, and asked that whoever had it should be allowed no health, or no sleep, until he brought it there; and I took this at the time for no more than the Lydney formula over again, and was glad to have ten new examples of it. There was only one thing that I could not account for. In every one of the ten the name of the Lady had been written, and in every one it had afterwards been beaten out with a blunt punch, so thoroughly that I could not get a letter of it. It was done, I think, by the people who wrote the tablets; the punch-marks lie under the folds. They had named her, as the form required, and had then been unwilling to leave the name behind them in writing.

The eleventh tablet had lain uppermost in the well, and was the best preserved. It was larger than the others, five inches by rather more than three, and it was in a better hand; and it had been pierced for a nail like the rest, but there had never been any nail in it. The hole was clean. I opened it upon the third evening, after dinner, with Mr Forster sitting by me. He had a tumbler of whisky and water at his elbow, and he had mixed another for me. He had no Latin beyond what a boy carries away from a grammar-school, and when I had the whole of it clear he said, ‘Read it out, man. Read it as they would have said it, and then give me the English.’

I ought to say here that I read Latin aloud rather well. I have taken some pains with it, and I have always held that an inscription is not understood until it has been heard. I read the tablet to him slowly, with the quantities, as I should read it to a class; and I then translated it, and this, as nearly as I can give it, is what it says:

‘To the Lady of this water’—and here the name is beaten out—‘What has been taken from this place is owed to this place. Whoever has it, whether man or woman, whether slave or free, whether he took it or had it of another, let water be no water to him. Let him drink and not have drunk, until he bring it again. Vitalis wrote this, who brought it again.’

The words which I have rendered ‘let water be no water to him’ are plain enough in the Latin, and I remember that I was pleased with them, and said that we had here the Bath formula turned inside out: at Bath the thief was to become water, and here water was to become nothing to the thief. Mr Forster said that it was a deuced unpleasant notion, and reached for his glass, and found that he had emptied it. So had I. Neither of us, I believe, had been aware of drinking while I read. He mixed two more, and we sat for an hour over the list, and he told me that the whole collection was promised to the Society’s museum at Newcastle, and that the cases would go in by the carrier upon the Thursday week.

I woke at about two in the morning with my mouth dry, and drank what was in the water-bottle, and lay down again. I was awake within a quarter of an hour, and I got up and filled the bottle at the ewer and drank the whole of that. I wish to be exact about what followed, because it is the only part of this account for which I can answer of my own knowledge. I do not mean that I was still thirsty after drinking. That happens to any man who has dined upon salt beef. I mean that the water went down my throat, and that I felt it cold in my mouth, and heavy within me, and that it did no part of the office of water. I might as well have swallowed so many pebbles. My tongue was as dry at the end of a quart as it had been at the beginning, and by four o’clock, when it grew light, there was no more water in the room, and I was standing at the window in my night-shirt, with the sash thrown up, catching the rain in my two hands.

Mr Forster was down before me. He looked as a man looks who has not slept, and he had the tea-pot at his own end of the table, and did not offer it. He said that the ham must have been salter than usual, and that he would speak about it. I said nothing of the tablet, and neither did he.

As soon as I decently could I walked up to the mile-castle, which is something under a mile from the house. I had no clear purpose. It was raining steadily, and the burn that I crossed was full, and I knelt down upon the stones and drank from it like a beast, and got up exactly as I had knelt. Dodd was upon the ground alone, wheeling earth. He was a gray, heavy, silent man of sixty, with a quarryman’s blue scars upon his hands; and he looked at me for some time, as I came through the south gate, before he put his barrow down.

‘You’ll have been at the lead, sir,’ he said.

I said that I had finished with it the night before.

‘Aye,’ said Dodd. He wiped his hands upon his breeches, and felt in his pocket, and brought out a penny, and held it out to me upon his palm. ‘I’m not to do it myself,’ he said. ‘The master’s forbidden it. But he never forbade you.’

I am a Master of Arts of my University, and I have written, with some severity, upon the credulity of the later Empire. I took the penny, and I went to the well-head and looked down. The water was at its twelve feet, and very still, and I could see my own head in it against the sky. Then it came to me that the penny was Dodd’s, and not mine, and that the tablet said nothing whatever of payment. It said *donec referat*, ‘until he bring it again.’ The men had kept nothing, and a penny had served them; but I had a thing of hers in my pocket. Upon my first evening Mr Forster had given me one of his coins for a keepsake, a little worn third brass of Tetricus, of which he had forty. I gave Dodd back his penny, and I took out the Tetricus and let it fall.

I did not hear it reach the water. I listened for it, and I saw no ring upon the surface, though I was looking straight down. But between the opening of my fingers and the time at which I ought to have heard it my mouth was wet, and I was no more thirsty than I am as I write this. I set that down as I should set down the weight of the tablet, and I do not explain it.

‘They never sound,’ said Dodd. ‘Not them that’s owing.’

I asked him then, and he told me, in very few words. The men had found in the first week that whoever handled what came out of the well was dry all the day after, and that no drink would touch it; and one of them, an old man from the North Tyne, had said that in his grandmother’s time nobody took so much as a can of water from a Wall well without leaving something for it. So they had paid a penny a day for what their hands had lifted, and had been easy. When the master fished the pennies up and set them in a jar they had been dry again, and worse, and the most of them had gone. I asked him why he himself had stayed. He said that he had put his own penny in upon the first morning of all, before anything was lifted, and had kept his hands off the finds from that day to this, and had only wheeled earth; and that somebody had to see the master through it.

I told Mr Forster the whole of this at luncheon, as plainly as I have told it here. I had better not try to give his answer in full. He was angry, as I should have been in his place: here was a scholar, whom he had fetched from the south of England to do him credit, talking like one of his own labourers. He said that he had paid for every spadeful that had come out of that hole, and had the wage-book to show for it, and owed no man a farthing. I said that I did not think it was a man that he owed. He said that if I had dropped a coin of his down the well he would thank me to remember that there were thirteen hundred and five remaining, and that they were going to Newcastle, where the public could see them, and not back into a hole in a field to oblige a quarryman. I noticed that he had a jug of water beside his plate, and that he filled his glass from it nine times while we talked.

I left upon the following day. I had no right to stay, and I had examination papers waiting for me; and I will confess that I was glad to go. He drove me to the station himself, and was civil, and a little ashamed of his temper; and the last thing I saw of him was his back, at the tap upon the platform at Bardon Mill.

His sister wrote to me three times. I had asked her to, privately, and I think that she had seen more than she ever said. The first letter was at the end of August. Her brother was not himself: he drank water all day long, and had a carafe carried into church; and the doctor had thought of diabetes, and had tested for sugar, and had found none. The cases, she added, had gone to Newcastle upon the Thursday, as arranged, and the Society had sent a very handsome letter of thanks. The second was in the middle of September. He had left off eating; he would have nothing in the house now but the water from the burn, which he sent a man for four times in a day; and he had been found at night in the scullery, with his mouth to the tap. He had written to Newcastle to ask for the loan of his collection for a fortnight, for the purpose of making drawings, and the Secretary had replied that it was already mounted and in the case, and that the Council did not meet until November. The third was a telegram, and I had it upon the twenty-ninth.

I was at Harlow Rigg upon the evening of the thirtieth, and he had been dead for two days. Dodd had found him. He had got out of the house in the night, by the garden door, and had walked up to the mile-castle in his dressing-gown and a pair of shooting boots; and he was lying upon the well-head, with his breast upon the stones, and his head and one arm hanging down inside. His purse was beside him, open and empty. I had from Dodd what I am now going to say, and afterwards from the doctor, who was fetched before the body was moved. The well, which had stood eleven feet deep in water through all that wet summer, was dry. They let down a lantern, and they saw the rock at the bottom of it, and the mud, and upon the mud they counted forty-one sovereigns, lying as they had fallen. By noon of the same day the water was in it again, and stood at twelve feet from the top.

The certificate says failure of the heart. The doctor was an old Army surgeon, and he told me in the dining-room, with the door shut, that if he had come upon that body in the Soudan he should have known what to call it; but that a man does not die of thirst in Northumberland in September, in a house with a cistern, and that he was not going to be the first to say so in writing.

Miss Forster asked me what she ought to do, and I told her. She had the jar of pennies emptied back into the well before the funeral; and when that was done she asked Dodd whether he would go down for her brother’s money. Dodd said that he would fill the well for her, but that he would not fish it. It was filled in that week with the whinstone from the old spoil-heaps, forty tons of it, and turfed over; and the forty-one sovereigns are under it, and all the pebbles. I think that she was right, and I helped her to it. But I have seen since what it was that we did. Whatever is in that glass case at Newcastle was owed at one place only, and there is now no such place for it to be brought to.

There is one thing more, and it is the thing that I have turned over oftenest. The men, who took with their own hands, were dry from the first day. Mr Forster, who only received what they took, had two summers of peace with it; and I had three days, with the Tetricus in my pocket. It did not reach either of us until the clause concerning him who ‘had it of another’ was said aloud in that room, in the tongue in which it was written—and it was I that said it. I can make nothing else of it.

It was after this that I wrote my letter. I asked that the whole should be buried upon the site of the well, which is the nearest thing that can now be done, and I do not know that it would serve. The collection is upon the upper floor of the museum, in a flat case by the north window, and is very well shown; the tablets are laid open, and each has a card, in my own writing, with the reading and a translation. I have not been to see it. I have given up reading inscriptions aloud—to my classes or alone—and I find that I can teach as well from the blackboard. I am told that the attendant in that room is a most respectable man, who has been with the Society eleven years, and who makes no complaint of his duties, except that he finds the place dusty.
