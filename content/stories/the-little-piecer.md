THERE are twenty minutes yet to the half-hour, and Sam has his fire made up and his can on the steam-pipe, so there is time for it if nobody interrupts. I would not tell it at all but for young Harry here. He was under number four carriage this forenoon with the wheels running, and he thinks that nobody saw him. I saw your heels, Harry. Sit down; nobody is going to clout you. Your minder will say that it saved him a stop, and so it did, about three-ha’p’orth. You will say that you were nimble, and so you were. Joe Garside was nimbler.

This was at Lawton’s, in the old mill by the canal, in the February of ’seventy-three, which is two-and-thirty years since. I was sixteen, and big piecer for Dan Wrigley on eleven and twelve. You will not remember Dan, Sam; he was before your time. He was a good minder and a near man. It was said of him that he would skin a flea for the hide and tallow, and that when he put a penny in the plate at chapel he waited to see what came back.

‘Wheels as are stood addle nowt,’ he used to say. He said it to me every day that I pieced for him, and twice on a Monday.

He had the cleanest pair of mules in the room and the fewest stops. Those two things do not go together by nature, as any spinner will tell you. They went together at Dan’s because Joe and I wiped down on the run.

For Sam’s sake, who believes that cotton spins itself so long as he keeps steam up, I had better say what that means. A mule carriage runs out from the roller-beam a matter of five feet, spinning as it comes. Then it runs in again and winds on. It does that three or four times in a minute, and the carriage is as long as the room. The fluff gathers underneath, and it must be got out, or it rides up into the yarn and the overlooker is down on you. The right way is to stop the wheels with the carriage out, and send the piecers under with a brush and a cloth. They run along bent double beneath the threads, from one end to the other; and when both are out, and the minder knows that they are out, he sets on again. But a minder is paid on the pound of yarn, and you have heard what Dan said about wheels. So there was another way. A lad nipped under while the carriage was coming out, and brushed as far as he could reach, and nipped back before it went in. Harry knows the way I mean.

There was a notice against it. It hung by the mule-room door in a frame, alongside the abstract of the Act, and Rule 9 said that any piecer found between the carriage and the roller-beam while the wheels were in motion should forfeit his week’s wage. Old Abel Lawton had it printed in Ashton. It kept him right with the inspector, and it cost him nothing beyond the printing, for nobody was ever found. The overlooker took care to be looking at something else.

Joe was nine years old, and a half-timer: the mill in the morning and school in the afternoon one week, and turn about the next. He was a little pale lad with a big head, the youngest of Martha Garside’s. She was a widow, and a winder in the same mill. He had half-a-crown a week. It was paid him at the office window on the Saturday with the rest of us, and stopped off Dan’s book; and he carried it home in his fist and gave it to his mother with his cap off, like a man paying his rent. He could go under a carriage and out again like a rat under a door. I never knew him catch a thread.

I shall be short with the Friday. It was a little before breakfast-time, dark yet, with the gas lit, and the room that thick with fly that you could not see the far end of a mule from the near. Dan had been spoken to the day before about dirty yarn, and for once in his life he stopped number twelve to have her wiped down properly. Joe went under at the far end and I went under at the near. I came out. I looked up the gate through the fly, and I saw a lad stood there in his shirt, barefoot like the rest of us.

‘Reet!’ I sang out, and Dan set on.

It was Wild’s lad, from the next pair, come across to borrow our oil-can. I am not going to tell you what there was to see. The carriage of number twelve went in as far as it could go, and that was not all the way.

On the Saturday at paying-time Martha came to the office window in her turn with the winders, and when she had her own money she asked for the lad’s. Seth Buckley was cashier then. He looked in his book, and then he looked at her over his spectacles.

‘It’s forfeit, Martha,’ he said. ‘Rule Nine.’

It had to be, do you see. If the firm had paid that half-crown, the firm would have been saying that the lad was where he had a right to be.

‘Thank yo’, Seth,’ said Martha. She put her own in her handkerchief and went home. A winder is easy come by, and she had three more to feed.

Before I was out of the yard that day Mr Platt, the manager, sent for me. He was very civil. He asked after my mother, who had four of us working at Lawton’s. Then he said that an inquest was a solemn thing, and that a lad of my age would do well to answer what he was asked and no more.

They held it on the Monday at the Commercial. Mr Platt brought the notice with him, frame and all, and read Rule 9 to the jury. Then I was asked had I seen the deceased come out from under the machine before it was started, and I said that I had seen a lad come out. Nobody asked me which lad. So it was written down that Joe had come out and gone under again of his own accord with the wheels moving, which was against the rule. The verdict was accidental death, and nobody to blame. He was buried on the Tuesday, at the chapel, and on the Wednesday at breakfast-time I heard him.

Sam will not know how breakfast was taken in a mule-room, never having earned one. The engine stopped at eight for the half-hour. The women and the children were turned out, by the Act, and the men and the big lads ate where they were, sat on a skip in the jenny-gate, with their cans off the steam-pipe. A mule-room with the wheels stopped is the quietest place I know. You can hear the gas. Dan and I were sat at the near end of number twelve with our bread, and there was a brush going. It was a scavenger’s brush, a short one, going along under the roller-beam the way a lad works it, three strokes and a shuffle. It was coming down number twelve from the far end.

‘Is that thee?’ says Dan.

‘How con it be me?’ I said. ‘I’m here.’

‘Then goo an’ see who it is, for I’m payin’ nob’dy to clean i’th’ breakfast hauf-hour.’

That was Dan. He thought of the wage first, and he was frightened after. I went up the gate, and I will own that I went slowly. There was nobody under number twelve from one end to the other. But the fluff was out. It was swept from under into the gate in little heaps, a yard apart, the way Joe always left it for me to gather.

By the Friday we had seen him. It was his feet first, going along under the carriage; and then one morning the whole of him, bent double beneath the threads with his big head down, sweeping. He never looked at anybody, and he never missed a morning. His clogs were by the mule-room door yet, where the piecers left them, for nobody had liked to shift them. He went past them at the half-hour’s end, and round the headstock, and that was all until the next day.

You will think that the room was frightened, and for a week it was. After that I am sorry to say that it was mostly jealous. Here was Dan Wrigley with his mules wiped down every morning of the week, and the wheels never stopped a minute for it, and nothing to pay. Jack Wild, on thirteen and fourteen, could not get over it.

‘Ther’s nobbut one mill i’ Lancashire,’ he said, ‘wheer they’n wark a lad after he’s deead, an’ we’re in it.’

‘He’s noan warkin’ for me,’ says Dan. ‘I never set him on.’

‘Thou’s three piecers to my two, an’ nobbut two to pay. They should stop thee for it at th’ window.’

Dan took it very ill. He was a week getting over the fright, and a fortnight getting used to the saving. After that he began to leave a piece of currant loaf on the creel-end at breakfast-time, where the lad would pass it. It was never touched. He left it every morning, notwithstanding, and I have known Dan Wrigley give less to the living. It was Dan, too, who said one morning that the lad was in a better place.

‘Then it’s a pity,’ says Wild, ‘as he connot stop theer of a mornin’.’

Mr Platt came up and stood in the middle of the room. ‘This is idleness,’ he said, ‘and women’s talk; and the next hand that names it in this mill will be stopped a shilling.’ So it was not named in the mill. It was named everywhere else between Mossley and Stalybridge. There were lads at the gate of a morning. ‘Is it reet,’ they shouted, ‘as Lawton’s han set a boggart on at hauf-a-crown?’

About the fourth week the sub-inspector came. They liked to come at meal-times, to see were the wheels stopped when they should be, and whether there were children in the rooms. He was a young man with a note-book. He walked into the mule-room at ten minutes past eight with Mr Platt behind him, talking, and he stopped at number twelve and pointed with his pencil.

‘There is a child cleaning under that machine,’ he said, ‘in the meal-time.’

Mr Platt looked. I will say this for him, that he saw what was there, for he went the colour of the fly.

‘Whose boy is that?’ says the inspector to Dan.

Dan had his mouth full. ‘He’s noan o’ mine,’ he said. ‘Mine’s wick.’

‘What does the man say?’

‘He says, sir,’ said Mr Platt, ‘that the boy is not in his employ.’ It was as near as Mr Platt could come to it.

Then the inspector called to the lad to come out and give his name. The lad swept on to the headstock, and round it, and that was the last of him for that morning. So the inspector wrote down, ‘Name refused,’ and the firm was summoned for employing a child in the meal-time.

I was not in court, and I had what follows from Seth Buckley, who carried the books. Mr Platt stood up and told the bench that there was no such child. The inspector asked for the register of children, and Seth handed it up. There was Joseph Garside, aged nine, with the surgeon’s certificate, and never struck off.

‘Is this the boy?’ says the chairman.

‘That boy is dead, your worship,’ says Mr Platt.

‘Then what is he doing on your register?’ says the inspector. ‘And if he is dead, who was under the machine?’

Mr Platt had the choice of telling three magistrates that he kept a boggart, or of paying forty shillings and the costs. He paid; and Joe swept on.

It was Jack Wild that began the reckoning. ‘Sithee,’ he said, ‘th’ lad’s on th’ book; we han that fro’ th’ magistrates. An’ he’s warkin’; we han that fro’ th’ inspector. Then he’s addlin’ wage. Hauf-a-crown a week, an’ th’ one they stopped. What’s that come to?’

‘He’s noan on my book,’ says Dan, very quick.

‘Nawe,’ says Wild. ‘He’s on Abel’s.’

After that it was chalked on the end of number twelve every Saturday, where Mr Platt could see it without being told: fifteen shillings, and seventeen-and-six, and a pound. Wild said that at the rate the lad was going Abel Lawton would owe him the mill by the Wakes.

Some of us thought to stop it in a kinder way. There was a gathering made round the room, coppers mostly, and I was sent round with the paper.

‘Put me down for sixpence,’ says Dan.

‘Write it big, Eli,’ says Wild. ‘Hoo’ll want to frame it.’

It came to four-and-ninepence, and I took it up to Martha’s on the Saturday night. She thanked the room, and she kept it, for she was no fool, and she had the burying to pay for. On the Monday at ten minutes past eight the brush was going under number twelve the same as ever.

‘It stands to reason,’ said Wild. ‘A gift’s a gift, an’ a wage is a wage. Th’ lad knows who he warked for.’

I have said little of the master. Old Abel was not a bad man, as masters go. He was at the same chapel as Dan and my mother, and had given it the harmonium. He believed that every word in his rule-book was as true as the other Book, since he had paid for the printing of it. He would not hear of the lad. But he did hear of the forty shillings; and one morning in the first week of May he came up into the mule-room himself at breakfast-time, with Mr Platt, to put an end to it.

He walked up number twelve gate in his boots—he was the one man in the room that wore any—and he stood about the middle of it, with his hands under his coat-tails.

‘Now then,’ he said, ‘wheer’s this lad?’

Nobody answered him, for the brush had started. It came down from the far end as it always did, three strokes and a shuffle, and the whole room watched it come, with their bread in their hands. The fluff came out into the gate in its little heaps. Then you could see him, bent double under the threads, with his head down. He came on until he was at the master’s boots, which were stood where he wanted to sweep, and there he stopped. He did not look up. He waited, the way a little piecer waits when a man is stood in his road, with his brush held ready. And old Abel did not shift. I think that he could not. He stood, and the lad waited, and the finger of the clock went round to the half-hour.

Then the engine started, as it is bound to do, and the shafting began to turn over our heads. Up and down the room the minders set on, one pair after another, and the carriages began to run. Dan stood at his headstock with his hand on the rod, and he looked down the gate at the master.

‘It’s hauf-past, maister,’ he said. ‘Mun I set on?’

Abel said nothing, and the carriages on either side of us were running.

‘Wheels as are stood addle nowt,’ says Dan. ‘An’ ther’s nobbut a lad under.’

I do not know to this day whether Dan said it against the master, or only as a minder who wanted to be spinning.

‘Let her stand,’ says Abel.

It was the first time in the memory of anybody there that a pair of mules had stood at Lawton’s on the master’s word, with steam up and nothing broken. He sent Mr Platt down to the office, and Mr Platt came back with Seth and the book. Seth, who was a careful man, asked what he was to put.

‘Put what’s chalked up,’ said Abel.

It was one pound seven and six; and Seth, of his own motion, put the week that was running on the top of it, which made thirty shillings. Then Abel lifted his feet, one and then the other, like a man coming out of clay, and stepped back out of the gate. Joe swept through the place where he had been standing, and on to the near end, and round the headstock.

Abel would have sent the money up to the house that night by Mr Platt. It was Seth who said that it was wage, and had better go through the window. So on the Saturday at paying-time Martha stood in her turn with the winders, and when she had her own, Seth called out, ‘Garside, Joseph, piecer,’ and counted thirty shillings into her hand. She counted it after him, which was her right.

‘Thank yo’, Seth,’ she said, the same as before.

On the Monday at breakfast-time there was no brush. By the Wednesday number twelve was as mucky underneath as any mule in the room, and Dan Wrigley had to stop his wheels and have her wiped down like a Christian. He left his bit of currant loaf on the creel-end for a week longer. Then he gave over, and ate it. The clogs went from the door the same Saturday, and I never heard who took them.

Dan minded eleven and twelve until his chest stopped him, and left more than anybody expected. Old Abel has been in the chapel yard these twelve years, under a stone that calls him a just master. Martha Garside lived to see her youngest minding a pair of his own at Lawton’s.

There is a piece that I have kept back, and Harry had better have it with the rest. I went up to Martha’s again that Saturday night, and I told her who it was that had shouted ‘Reet,’ and what I had said at the Commercial. She was getting the youngest to bed.

‘I know that, lad,’ she said. ‘I knew it at th’ inquest.’

I asked her why she had never spoken. ‘What for?’ she said. ‘To get thee stopped an’ all?’ She was a good while over the child’s buttons. Then she said, ‘Thou’ll look, another time.’

I said that I would, and I have. I count my piecers out by their names before I set on, every time, and I have been laughed at for it by better spinners than Harry’s minder.

They stop the wheels for cleaning at Lawton’s now. It is on the notice, ten minutes of a Tuesday and a Friday, and the time paid for. The Act had been asking them to do it for thirty years. I will not say that it was Joe who got it done; but it was not the Act.

That is the half-hour, Sam, near enough, and your engine will be wanting you. Fetch your can, Harry. You are with me this afternoon; I have spoken to your minder.
