AT ten minutes past eleven on the night of the fourteenth of November, the last car but one left the Nether Edge terminus with three passengers inside and none on top. The conductor, a steady man named Hobson with eleven years’ service, was prepared to swear to this before any coroner in England, and in the event he had to. For when the car reached Moorhead there were four inside; and the fourth had not paid.

I give the facts as they came out in evidence. I was in court on both days for my paper, at the reporters’ table, not six feet from the witnesses, and I have my notes before me as I write.

The year was 1893. It was a horse car, as all the Sheffield cars were then: a pair of greys, a driver in front, the conductor on the step behind, two benches inside facing one another, and a stair to the roof. One oil lamp burned in a box at the forward end. From Nether Edge to Moorhead is something under two miles, nearly all of it downhill, and the time allowed was twenty minutes.

The three who got in at the terminus were these.

Mr. Josiah Marples, fifty-eight, retired pawnbroker, of Nether Edge. He took the far corner on the left-hand side, next the lamp.

Miss Ada Furniss, twenty-two, milliner’s assistant, going home from her mother’s. She sat opposite to him.

Mr. Albert Wragg, nineteen, clerk at a steel works in Savile Street, who had been seeing a young lady to her door. He sat by the entrance.

Hobson knew the first two by sight, and punched three twopenny tickets. Mr. Marples, who was a man of regular habits, took out his watch as the car started, and said, “Ten past. You are to your time, conductor.”

They were the last words that anyone heard him speak in the ordinary way. At Moorhead he was dead in his corner.

The inquest was opened two days later, in the long room of an hotel near the top of the Moor. The room held eighty. There were two hundred more upon the stairs and in the street, for the thing had been in the evening papers; and I may say, without boasting, that it had been in mine first.

John Hobson, the conductor, was sworn.

He said that the car did not stop between the terminus and Moorhead, the night being cold and nobody about. Coming down Cemetery Road he looked in through the glass of the door, to count his passengers against his way-bill, as the rule was; and there were four.

“Where was the fourth sitting?”—“In the far corner, sir, on the left. Next to Mr. Marples.”

“Between Mr. Marples and the end of the car?”—“No, sir. On this side of him. Close up.”

“Had the car stopped?”—“No, sir.”

“Could a man have got on while it was moving?”—“He could, sir, at that pace. But he must have come past me on the step, and opened the door; and that door was never opened. It sticks. I should have heard it.”

“Describe him.”

Hobson’s description was taken down by the police, and was afterwards printed upon a bill. I copy it from the bill.

“A man of about thirty years, five feet nine or ten inches, strongly made. Dark hair, worn long. No hat. Dressed in a shirt and trousers only, the shirt open at the neck. Barefooted. The upper part of the left ear wanting. Hair and clothing wet through.”

“Wet?” said the Coroner. “It was a dry night.”—“It was, sir. He was running with it. There was water going down the floor of the car towards the horses. I could see the lamp in it.”

“Did you go in to take his fare?”—“I went in, sir.”

“And you took it?”—“No, sir.”

“Why not?”

Hobson was some time in answering. He said at last: “I got as far as Mr. Wragg. I have had drunken men in my car, sir, and men in fits, and once I had a man die. I never had anything in it like that. I came out, and I shut the door, and I stood on the step with my back to it till we were at Moorhead.”

“You are a conductor of eleven years’ service, and you left a man in your car without a ticket?”—“I did, sir. I should do it again.”

I find that I have written against this in my notes the one word, “Sensation.”

William Platts, the driver, said that he had not pulled up between the terminus and Moorhead, and that nobody had signalled to him. Below the Cemetery gates the horses had taken charge, and he had not been able to hold them for a quarter of a mile; they were in a lather when he stopped, upon a night of frost, and one of them had not been right since.

“They knew,” said Platts.

The Coroner told him that that was not evidence.

Miss Furniss gave her evidence in so low a voice that she was twice asked to repeat it, and she was allowed to sit.

She said that she had been tired, and had been looking at her hands, when she felt it turn cold in the car, “as if somebody had opened a cellar.” Then there was a smell, which she did not like to describe; but being pressed, she said that it was like the river at Lady’s Bridge in August.

She looked up, and there was a man beside Mr. Marples who had not been there before, and the water was running out of his hair on to Mr. Marples’s shoulder. Mr. Marples had his eyes shut.

“Was he asleep?”—“No, sir. His lips were going.”

“Did the man look at you?”—“He never looked at anybody but Mr. Marples. He was turned right round in his seat to look at him. Close. The way you would look at a clock.”

“Did he speak?”—“No, sir.”

“Did he touch the deceased?”

The witness did not answer. The question was put again.

“He took hold of his wrist, sir. The right one. He was not rough. He took hold, and he kept hold, all the way down Cemetery Road.”

“Why did you not leave the car?”—“I should have had to go past them.”

Albert Wragg said that he was sitting by the door, with his back to the horses, and that he had seen the man’s face when the car went under the gas-lamp at the Cemetery gates.

“Describe it.”

“It was the colour of a candle. The eyes were open. I watched them, and they never shut. Not once, from the Cemetery gates to the bottom of the hill. There was mud in the corners of the mouth.”

“Did you hear anything said?”—“Mr. Marples spoke. Twice.”

“What did he say?”—“He said, ‘I couldn’t hold thee, George.’ And then, a good while after, ‘There was only the one rope.’”

“To whom did he say it?”—“To him, sir.”

“Did the other make any answer?”—“He held on.”

At Moorhead, Hobson rang off, and Miss Furniss and Mr. Wragg left the car a good deal quicker than the regulations allow. Mr. Marples did not move. The fourth passenger was not there.

Hobson had never left the step, and nobody had come past him; and there is no other door.

Police-constable Dawson, who was on duty at Moorhead, said that there were fifty people round the car before he had his lantern lit, and upwards of two hundred by the time the hand-ambulance came. They stood six deep, and they were looking, he said, at the water, which was coming out under the door and dripping from the step.

He examined the car, and found the cushion of the left-hand bench soaked through for the width of one man, beside the place where the deceased sat, and water upon the floor to the amount of a quart or more. He noticed the smell. There were no wet marks upon the roadway, of feet or of anything else.

The police surgeon said that the deceased was a stout man with a fatty and diseased heart, and that death was from syncope, and was consistent with a severe shock. The clothing was dry, except the left shoulder and sleeve of the overcoat, and the right cuff.

“Were there any marks of violence?”—“There were five bruises round the right wrist. Four in a row upon the back of it, and one upon the front.”

“Made by what?”—“By a hand. They were made during life, and within the hour.”

A juryman asked whether it would need a strong grip.

“I have seen such marks once before,” said the surgeon. “They were upon a man taken out of the canal at Tinsley. He had been held by another man, who drowned with him.”

Nobody in that room made a sound. I looked up from my book, and I saw that the people on the stairs had taken their hats off.

The Inspector then read the list of the property found upon the deceased, with the values.

One silver watch and guard, £2. One purse, containing £3 4s. 6d. One gold signet ring, 30s. One pair of spectacles in a case. One bunch of keys. One tram ticket, 2d.

Nothing appeared to have been taken, and the police did not think that robbery was the motive; but they asked for an adjournment, in order to find the fourth passenger, and the inquest was adjourned for a week.

The bill was out that night at every police-station and car-shed in Sheffield. “WANTED, to give evidence,” it began, and then the description as I have given it.

In the week that followed the man was seen, by my own count, upon nine cars, in two public-houses, and at Rotherham; and none of them was he. My paper sold out three editions on the Thursday.

The room was fuller on the second day than on the first, and the police had nothing to report.

Then an elderly woman, who had sat through the first day at the back of the court, got up and said that she wished to be heard.

She gave her name as Sarah Beal, sixty-one, widow, of Neepsend Lane, and said that she had read the bill.

“Do you know the man described in it?”—“I do.”

“Who is he?”—“George Bower. He lodged with Marples in Neepsend Lane, three doors from us. He was a file-cutter. The top of his left ear had been gone since he was a lad. A horse had it.”

“Where is he now?”—“He was drowned, sir. In the Flood. On the night of the eleventh of March, eighteen sixty-four. They never found him.”

The Coroner told the witness that she must confine herself to what she had seen with her own eyes. She said that she intended to.

She said that the water woke them, and that she and her husband went to the chamber window. The gas-lamp across the lane was still alight, and burned until the water put it out. The front of Marples’s house was gone; and there were two men in the water, in their shirts, as they had got out of bed, who had hold of the lamp-post and of one another, with the water up to their arm-pits and rising.

Her husband made the clothes-line fast to the bed, and threw the end, and Marples caught it. Bower had by then lost his hold of the post, and had Marples by the wrist.

“Go on.”

“It was a clothes-line, sir. It would never have held two. My husband shouted as much. Marples loosed him. He took his other hand to it, and he undid the fingers, one at a time. I saw him do it, by the lamp. Then George went down the lane on the water, upon his back, looking up. He never called out.”

“You saw his face?”—“I did, sir. That is why I have come.”

“Did you speak of this to anybody at the time?”—“My husband said we should not. Marples came in at our window, and sat on our bed, and never spoke a word till it was light. He was a young man. What was he to do? I have never held it against him. But I never could abide the sight of him after; and I think he knew it, for he flitted to the other side of the town that same year, and I have not set eyes on him from that day to this.”

“And you tell the jury that the man described in this bill is the man whom you saw drowned twenty-nine years ago?”

“I tell them what his ear was, sir, and his shirt, and that he had neither hat nor boots, and that he was thirty-one. I say nowt else. The rest is for you gentlemen.”

The jury were out five-and-twenty minutes.

They found that the deceased died from failure of the heart, accelerated by fright; but that by what means the fright was occasioned there was no evidence to show. They added a rider, calling the attention of the Tramways Company to the insufficient lighting of its cars.

The Coroner asked whether they wished to say anything as to the fourth passenger.

“We do not,” said the foreman.

The Company put a second lamp in the Nether Edge cars before Christmas. It fined Hobson one shilling for failing to collect a fare. He paid it the same day, and he told me that he thought it cheap.
