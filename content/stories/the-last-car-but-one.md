I HAVE been a newspaper man for three-and-twenty years, and in that time I have written up, at a moderate computation, fourteen haunted houses, a spectral nun, and a black dog the size of a calf. I mention it that you may know the sort of person you are dealing with. I never set foot in two of the houses. The nun turned out to be a laundress with a grievance; and I made the dog myself, in the train between Sheffield and Chesterfield, there being nothing else to send and the paper going to press at four. So when I tell you that I do not believe in ghosts, you will understand that I speak as a conjuror speaks of magic. I have put the rabbit into the hat too many times with my own hands.

A ghost, in my trade, is a thing that comes in during a slack week. It is commonly brought by a landlady, or by a constable who would be glad of half-a-crown, and it is worth to the man who writes it up about a penny-farthing a line, and something more if London takes it. I have never known one that would bear looking into, and I have never looked into one that I was not paid for. I say all this without shame. It is as well that you should have it before you, because I am now going to tell you of one that I had no hand in making, and that I cannot explain.

It happened at Sheffield, in the November of 1886, when I was eight-and-twenty and on the staff of an evening paper there. This is how my report of it began.

At ten minutes past eleven on the night of the fourteenth of November, the last car but one left the Nether Edge terminus with three passengers inside and none on top. The conductor, a steady man named Hobson with eleven years’ service, was prepared to swear to this before any coroner in England, and in the event he had to. For when the car reached Moorhead there were four inside; and the fourth had not paid.

You will want to know how I came by it. I was walking home across Moorhead from a smoking-concert, of which I had a notice to write, and the concert had been of a kind that made me in no hurry to begin, when I saw a crowd of some fifty people about the Nether Edge car. I have never in my life gone past a crowd. A crowd means that something has happened to somebody else, which is the whole of what newspapers are for; and this was a crowd of a sort that I knew from a good way off, for it was perfectly quiet, and everybody in it was looking the same way.

They were not looking at the dead man, who was inside the car and out of their sight. They were looking at the water, which was coming out from under the door and dripping off the step into the road, on a night that was as dry as a bone and freezing hard.

The dead man was a Mr. Josiah Marples, a retired pawnbroker, of Nether Edge, fifty-one years of age and stout. He was sitting in the far corner on the left-hand side, under the lamp, with his eyes shut and his hat on. I had seen a fair number of people who had died suddenly, and I should have said that this one had been badly frightened first; but I did not put that in, because a reporter who gives opinions in the first edition is generally found giving explanations in the second.

The constable gave me the name and address. I could get nothing out of the conductor, for Hobson was sitting on the kerb with his head in his hands, and when I asked him the usual question he looked up at me and did not answer. I took him to be in drink. I went to the office and wrote the paragraph that I have given you, and I thought it a tolerably neat thing, and worth perhaps four shillings; and then I went to bed, and I may say that I slept.

* * *

The inquest was opened on the sixteenth, in the long room of an hotel near the top of the Moor. The room would hold eighty at a pinch, and there were two hundred more upon the stairs and in the street. I put this down to my paragraph, and was a good deal pleased with myself.

I went to that inquest in a thoroughly comfortable frame of mind. I thought that I knew what I should hear. A tram conductor in whose car a passenger has died has the best of reasons for having seen a mysterious stranger. A young woman who is going to be in the newspapers will remember anything that is asked of her. As for the water, I supposed that there was a horse-trough at the bottom of it, or a bucket. I had my opening written in my head before the Coroner had taken his seat, and it was a facetious one.

Hobson was called first. He was a spare, grizzled man, with something of the old soldier about him, and he gave his evidence as though every word of it were coming out of his own pocket. He said that he had three inside at the terminus, and punched three twopenny tickets; that Mr. Marples had taken out his watch as they started, and remarked that they were to their time; and that the car did not stop anywhere between Nether Edge and Moorhead, the night being cold and nobody about. Coming down Cemetery Road he looked in through the glass of the door, to count his passengers against his way-bill, as the rule was; and there were four.

I shall give you the next part as it stands in my note-book, question and answer.

“Where was the fourth sitting?”—“In the far corner, sir. Next to Mr. Marples, on the near side of him. Close up.”

“Could a man have got on while the car was moving?”—“He could, sir, at that pace. But he must have come past me on the step, and opened the door; and that door was never opened. It sticks. I should have heard it.”

“Describe him.”—“A man about thirty, sir, five foot nine or ten, and well set up. Dark hair, and long. He had no hat. He had a shirt on, and his trousers, and that was all. He was barefoot. The top of his left ear was gone. He was wet through.”

“Wet? It was a dry night.”—“It was, sir. He was running with it. There was water going down the floor of the car towards the horses. I could see the lamp in it.”

“Did you go in to take his fare?”—“I went in, sir.”

“And you took it?”—“No, sir.”

“Why not?”

Now a witness who is making up his story has his answer ready at this point, and it is generally a good one. Hobson had none. He stood for the better part of a minute looking at the Coroner’s ink-pot; and then he said that he had got as far as the young gentleman by the door, and that he had had drunken men in his car in his time, and men in fits, and once a man who died, but that he had never had anything in it like that; and that he had come out again, and shut the door, and stood on the step with his back to it until they were at Moorhead.

“You are a conductor of eleven years’ service,” said the Coroner, “and you left a man in your car without a ticket?”

“I did, sir,” said Hobson. “I should do it again.”

I had been on the point of writing “Laughter” in my book, from habit. I found that I had written “Sensation” instead; and I remember that I sat and looked at the word for some little time, as if somebody else had put it there.

The driver came next, a man named Platts, who said that he had not pulled up between the terminus and Moorhead, and that nobody had signalled to him. Below the Cemetery gates his horses had taken charge, and he had not been able to hold them for a quarter of a mile; they were in a lather when he stopped, upon a night of frost, and one of them had not been right since. “They knew,” said Platts; and the Coroner, who was a sensible man, told him that that was not evidence. It was not, of course. But I have had a good deal to do with horses at one time and another, in the way of business, and I have yet to meet the horse that would perjure itself.

Then they called Miss Furniss, from whom I had promised myself some entertainment. She was a milliner’s assistant of two-and-twenty, a plain, decent girl with red hands, and she gave her evidence in so low a voice that she was twice asked to repeat it, and was at last allowed to sit. It was plain that she would have given a week’s wages to be anywhere else, and that is not at all the way of a young woman who is inventing.

She said that she had been tired, and had been looking at her hands, when she felt it turn cold in the car, “as if somebody had opened a cellar.” Then there was a smell, which she did not like to describe; but being pressed, she said that it was like the river at Lady’s Bridge in August. She looked up, and there was a man beside Mr. Marples who had not been there before, and the water was running out of his hair on to Mr. Marples’s shoulder. Mr. Marples had his eyes shut, but he was not asleep, for his lips were going. The man never once looked at her. He was turned right round in his seat to look at Mr. Marples, close, “the way you would look at a clock.”

“Did he touch the deceased?”

She did not answer, and the question had to be put to her again.

“He took hold of his wrist, sir. The right one. He was not rough. He took hold, and he kept hold, all the way down Cemetery Road.”

“Why did you not leave the car?”—“I should have had to go past them.”

I have tried since then, in the way of trade, to get as much as that into eight words of my own, and I have not managed it.

The third passenger was a clerk of nineteen named Wragg, who was sulky, I think because the father of a certain young lady was in court and now knew where he had been until eleven at night. He had been sitting by the door, with his back to the horses, and he had seen the man’s face when the car went under the gas-lamp at the Cemetery gates. He said that it was the colour of a candle. He said that the eyes were open, and that he had watched them, and that they never shut, not once, from the Cemetery gates to the bottom of the hill; and that there was mud in the corners of the mouth.

“Did you hear anything said?”—“Mr. Marples spoke. Twice.”

“What did he say?”—“He said, ‘I couldn’t hold thee, George.’ And then, a good while after, ‘There was only the one rope.’”

“Did the other make any answer?”—“He held on.”

The police surgeon said that the deceased had a fatty and diseased heart, and that death was from syncope, and was consistent with a severe shock. The clothing was dry, except the left shoulder and sleeve of the overcoat, and the right cuff. There were five bruises round the right wrist, four in a row upon the back of it and one upon the front, which had been made by a hand, during life, and within the hour. A juryman asked whether it would have needed a strong grip.

“I have seen such marks once before,” said the surgeon. “They were upon a man taken out of the canal at Tinsley, who had been held by another man that drowned with him.”

I am not a fanciful person, and I had gone there to be amused. I can only tell you that nobody in that room made a sound, and that when I looked up from my book I saw that the people on the stairs had taken their hats off. I have seen that done for a coffin. I never before saw it done for a piece of medical evidence.

After that the Inspector read out the list of what had been found upon the deceased, with the values, as is always done; and I took it down, as I always do, for it is a curious fact that the public, which will skip a leading article, will read an inventory to the last halfpenny. There was a silver watch and guard, put at two pounds; a purse, with three pounds four and sixpence in it; a gold signet ring, thirty shillings; a pair of spectacles in a case; a bunch of keys; and one tram ticket, twopence. Nothing had been taken. The police did not think that robbery came into it; but they asked for a week, in order to find the fourth passenger, and they got it.

* * *

I will confess that I had a hand in the wording of the bill, the Inspector being a better policeman than he was a writer. “WANTED, to give evidence,” it began, and then came Hobson’s description, ear and all. It was out that night at every police-station and car-shed in Sheffield.

In the week that followed that man was seen, by my own count, upon nine cars, in two public-houses, and at Rotherham. I interviewed most of the people who saw him, and I came away from every one of them easier in my mind than I had come away from the inquest. They were the kind of witness that I understood. Each of them had seen a little more than the last, and two of them asked me what the paper paid. We sold out three editions on the Thursday, and I made four pounds eleven in lineage from the London papers, which was more than I had ever made in a week before.

The room was fuller on the second day than on the first, and the police had nothing to report. Then an elderly woman, who had sat through the whole of the first day at the back of the court, stood up and said that she wished to be heard.

She gave her name as Sarah Beal, fifty-four, widow, of Neepsend Lane, and said that she had read the bill. She knew the man. He was George Bower, a file-cutter, who had lodged with Marples in Neepsend Lane, three doors from her; and the top of his left ear had been gone since he was a lad, when a horse had it. Being asked where he was now, she said that he was drowned. He was drowned in the Flood, on the night of the eleventh of March, eighteen sixty-four, and they never found him.

I must tell you that I knew Neepsend Lane. I was born within a stone’s-throw of it, and I was six years old on the night that she was speaking of.

The Coroner told her that she must keep to what she had seen with her own eyes, and she said that she intended to. She said that the water woke them, and that she and her husband went to the chamber window. The gas-lamp across the lane was still alight, and burned until the water put it out. The front of Marples’s house was gone; and there were two men in the water, in their shirts, as they had got out of bed, who had hold of the lamp-post and of one another, with the water up to their arm-pits and rising. Her husband made the clothes-line fast to the bed, and threw the end, and Marples caught it. Bower had by then lost his hold of the post, and had Marples by the wrist.

“It was a clothes-line, sir,” she said. “It would never have held two. My husband shouted as much. Marples loosed him. He took his other hand to it, and he undid the fingers, one at a time. I saw him do it, by the lamp. Then George went down the lane on the water, upon his back, looking up. He never called out.”

“Did you speak of this to anybody at the time?”—“My husband said we should not. Marples came in at our window, and sat on our bed, and never spoke a word till it was light. He was a young man. What was he to do? I have never held it against him. But I never could abide the sight of him after; and I think he knew it, for he flitted to the other side of the town that same year, and I have not set eyes on him from that day to this.”

“And you tell the jury that the man described in this bill is the man whom you saw drowned two-and-twenty years ago?”

“I tell them what his ear was, sir, and his shirt, and that he had neither hat nor boots, and that he was thirty-one. I say nowt else. The rest is for you gentlemen.”

I was carried up to the garret that night upon my father’s back, and I do not remember the water at all. What I remember is the noise, and afterwards the smell, which lay in our street until midsummer, and which was the smell that Miss Furniss had been pressed to describe. I had not thought of it for years. I sat at the reporters’ table and took down what Mrs. Beal said, and my hand was not so steady as I should have liked it to be.

The jury were out five-and-twenty minutes. They found that the deceased died from failure of the heart, accelerated by fright, but that by what means the fright was occasioned there was no evidence to show; and they added a rider, calling the attention of the Tramways Company to the insufficient lighting of its cars. The Coroner asked them whether they wished to say anything as to the fourth passenger.

“We do not,” said the foreman.

* * *

That ought to be the end of it, and for the public it was. For me there was a week more.

My editor was a man who never let a good thing go while there was a column left in it, and he wanted something for the Saturday: “A Ride on the Haunted Car, by One who does not Believe.” I had written that article, under one heading or another, a dozen times. There is a recipe for it. You go to the place at the proper hour; nothing happens; and you are funny about it for a column and a turn. I was glad of the chance, for I had not been comfortable since the Tuesday, and I thought that a column of facetiousness was exactly the medicine I wanted.

On the Thursday night, therefore, I went up to Nether Edge, took a glass of something hot at the house by the terminus, and got into the last car but one at ten minutes past eleven. Hobson was the conductor. He had asked to be kept upon that car, I was told, sooner than have it said that he was afraid of it. Nobody else got in, for the receipts upon that road after ten at night had gone down to nothing. I sat by the door, in the place where Mr. Wragg had sat. I am a sceptic; but I did not see that scepticism required me to sit in the far corner.

I had my note-book on my knee. I noted that there was one lamp, and that it smoked; that there was straw upon the floor; and that the Company let its panels to a maker of mustard. I had the first three sentences of my column composed by the time we were at the Cemetery gates, and they were good ones.

I did not look at the far corner. I should like to be able to give you a better account of that than I can. I did not look; I knew perfectly well that I was not looking; and I read the advertisement for mustard, over and over, from the Cemetery gates to the bottom of the hill. It had turned exceedingly cold, which I put down to the hour. The horses were going a great deal faster than the Company’s time-table.

I could see Hobson, however, upon the step. He looked in through the glass of the door, to count his passengers against his way-bill, as the rule was. I saw his lips move. Then I saw him begin again, and count a second time.

At Moorhead I got down from that car rather more quickly than the regulations allow, and I said something cheerful to Hobson about its being one to the unbelievers. He made no answer. He held out his hand to me, and there were two pennies in it.

“What is this?” I said.

“Your fare, sir. I had sooner not have taken money for that journey.”

“How many did you make it, Hobson?”

“I had sooner not say, sir.”

I walked home. It was not until I sat down to take my boots off that I found they were wet through, and my socks with them, on a night when there had not been a drop of rain in Sheffield for nine days.

I did not write the column. I told my editor that nothing had happened, which was true so far as it went, and he said that nothing was of no use to him at any price. The Company put a second lamp in the Nether Edge cars before Christmas. It also fined Hobson a shilling for failing to collect a fare on the night of the fourteenth; and he paid it, and told me that he thought it cheap, and left the Company’s service in the New Year.

I have explained a great many things in three-and-twenty years, and I could explain this. I could say that Hobson miscounted, and that a milliner’s girl of two-and-twenty will see whatever she is afraid of, and that an old woman with a long memory read a bill in a shop window. I have written it out in that form more than once. It reads very well, and I do not believe a word of it.

I have the two pennies still. I have never liked to spend them.
