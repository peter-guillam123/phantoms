I HAVE been asked more than once to write down what happened at the Cocos station in the year after the cyclone, and I have always put it off; partly because I am an operator and no writer, and partly because I had nothing to offer by way of explanation, and a story without one is thought, I know, to be a poor thing. I have none to offer now. But the matter has got about the Company’s stations, as such things will, and I find that it gains a little every time it goes round the mess-table; so that it seems better that one who was there should put down what occurred, and no more. Mr Mackellar, who was Superintendent at the time, and has since gone home on his pension, has been good enough to say that he has no objection.

I must first say something of the place and of the work, for the benefit of those—and I suppose they are most people—who send a telegram to Australia without asking themselves by what road it goes.

The Cocos or Keeling Islands lie in the Indian Ocean, some seven hundred miles from Java, and a great deal farther from anywhere else. They are a ring of low coral islets about a lagoon, none of them more than a few feet above the tide, and all of them covered with cocoa-nut palms. They belong to a Mr Ross, whose family has held them for three generations, and who lives on Home Island among his Malays in a patriarchal way, and pays them in money of his own making, which is printed upon sheepskin. On the island next to his the Company has its station. The charts call it Direction Island. The Malays call it Pulu Tikus, which is Rat Island; and I have always thought theirs the better name.

Three cables come ashore there: one from Perth in Western Australia, one from Batavia, and one from Rodriguez, which goes on to Mauritius and so to Africa. A message from Sydney to London may therefore pass through our hands, and at certain hours of the night a great many do. It does not go through of itself, as people suppose. It comes to us as a wavering line of ink upon a paper tape, which we call the slip; a clerk reads it off, and another sends it on by hand over the next section; and so it travels, much as buckets are passed at a fire. For this reason the instrument-room is never empty. There is a watch kept in it day and night, and has been since the station was opened, and I suppose will be until somebody finds a better way of sending messages than along the bottom of the sea.

Every station keeps what is called the office diary. It is a large ruled book, and the senior clerk of each watch writes it up before he goes off: the hour at which he took over, and from whom; any interruption or fault, with the time to the minute; the tests; the barometer and the wind; and the hour at which he was relieved. He then puts his initials to it. I may say that it is kept by Greenwich time, as all the Company’s business is, which at Cocos is some six hours and a half behind the sun; but to save confusion I shall give the island time here. It is very dry reading. The Superintendent goes through it once a week and initials the foot of each page, and after that I do not think any one turns back to it at all, unless the Company’s Inspector should come, which at Cocos is seldom. I mention these particulars because the whole of what I have to tell turns upon that book.

I came to Cocos on the 20th of May, by the Company’s steamer from Batavia, to take the place of Mr James Hollis, senior clerk of the middle watch, who had gone home. I had known Hollis for the better part of twenty years. We were probationers together in Cornwall; we had shared a bungalow at Zanzibar; and since then we had written to one another, not often, but regularly, as men do in the Service who are not very likely to meet. He had been nine years at Cocos—which is longer than is good for any man—and for nearly the whole of that time he had kept the middle watch, from midnight until six, by his own choice. As it fell out, I did meet him. The Company’s steamer, which had brought him in from the islands, was to take me out to them; and as she lay some days at Batavia, we dined together ashore. I thought him aged, and a good deal quieter than he used to be; but he was in good spirits at the prospect of England, and talked of a sister at Exeter, and of what he should do with a garden. I saw him on board the Singapore boat on the morning of the 10th. He had left Cocos on the 3rd. I am particular as to these dates, because a great deal hangs by them.

There were at that time about thirty of us on the island, taking clerks, mechanicians, and servants together. We lived in long bungalows raised upon piles, with a verandah all round, and we were, I think, as comfortable as men can be who have the same faces at every meal. There was a tennis-court of asphalt, which held the rain in puddles; and it was as well that something did, for there is no water in the ground of that island fit to drink, and all that we used was caught off the roofs. The mails came when the steamer came. For the rest there was the lagoon to sail upon, and the crabs to watch, which go about the beaches in regiments; and there were the rats.

Of the rats I must say a word, because they come into the story. They live in the heads of the palms, sixty and seventy feet from the ground, and bring up their young there, and feed upon the nuts; and at night they come down. They have always had the run of the instrument-room, where the lamps draw the insects, and where they go along the beams over the tables as if the place had been built for them. Nobody troubles about them. But Hollis had a fancy concerning them. A man who keeps the middle watch for nine years must have something to occupy his mind, and his amusement was to count the rats that showed themselves during the watch, and to enter the number in the diary, after the wind—‘Rats, 3,’ or ‘Rats, nil,’ as it might be. It was quite irregular. I was told that Mackellar had spoken to him about it more than once in the early years, and had then given it up, seeing that the entries were otherwise the best kept on the station; and that the mess had at one time got up a sweepstake upon the week’s total. No one else had ever entered them.

I was to take over the middle watch on the 22nd. On the 21st, having nothing to do, I went across to the office and asked for the diary, meaning to read back a month or two, and so learn the ways of the three circuits—which sections gave trouble, and at what hours. It is a thing I have always done at a new station, and I can recommend it.

I began at the first of April and read forward. The middle watch was in Hollis’s hand throughout, as I expected. It was a small upright hand, with the sevens crossed in the foreign manner, which he had learnt, I believe, when he was at the Lisbon station as a young man; and each watch ended in the same way, with the wind, and the rats, and two short strokes ruled beneath. I came to the 3rd of May, which was the last night he kept, and I turned the leaf, and had read on for some little way before I understood that I was reading him still.

I went back, thinking that I had mistaken the dates. I had not. The middle watch of the 4th was in the same hand, and of the 5th, and so on to the 21st, which was that same morning’s. The other watches for those days were in the writing of the men who had kept them, and I knew some of them already. The middle watch was Hollis’s, initials and all, down to the two strokes; and against every night, after the wind, there stood the number of the rats. Now on the 10th of that month, as I have said, I had myself seen James Hollis go up the side of a steamer in Batavia roads, eight hundred miles away.

I took the book to young Finch, who had kept the middle watch since Hollis left, being the next in seniority, and I asked him who had written it up. He said, rather shortly, that he had, at six o’clock every morning. I laid it before him, open at the 12th. I have seldom seen a man so much put out. He said several times over that it was not his writing, which I knew very well; and then he said that he could tell me what he had written on that particular morning, because Perth had been off for forty minutes with a fault at their end, and he had been at some pains to get the times right. They were all there, to the minute. There was nothing amiss with the entry except the hand that it was in, and the rats, which he declared he had never entered in his life, nor so much as looked at.

We went together to the Superintendent. Mr Mackellar was an Aberdeen man, who had been thirty years in the Service, and did not expect to be surprised by anything in it. He looked at the pages for a long while through his glass. Two of them he had initialled himself. He then said that somebody was playing the fool, and that he would know who it was; and that afternoon he had every man on the station write out the entry of the 12th from dictation, and compared them. He was no wiser when he had done. There was no one on the island who could have imitated that hand for a line together, let alone for eighteen nights; and—what weighed more with me—there was no one who would have thought it worth his while. A practical joke is played in order to be found out; and this had been going on for near three weeks in a book which nobody read.

I am, I hope, a methodical man, and I went about the business as I should have gone about a fault upon the line, which is by dividing the circuit, and then dividing it again, until one knows in which part of it the trouble lies.

On the 22nd I kept my first watch. At six I wrote it up in my own hand and initialled it, and handed over to Mr Lowe, and I stood by while he read what I had written. It was in my writing then—he will bear me out. I went to my breakfast. At eight I came back with Mackellar, and it was in Hollis’s. It was what I had written, word for word and figure for figure: that I had taken over from Mr Dale at midnight, that Rodriguez had been weak from half-past one until two, that Batavia had cleared at ten minutes to three. Only the hand was changed; and there was one thing added. After the wind there stood, ‘Rats, 4.’ I had entered no rats. But I had counted them that night, to see—and there had been four.

Lowe said that the book had lain at his elbow from six o’clock, that nobody had touched it, and that he had not looked at it again himself, having no occasion.

On the night following I wrote nothing whatever. With Mackellar’s leave I locked the diary in the drawer of the testing-table at six, and kept the key in my pocket, he having the only other; and at eight the two of us opened the drawer together. The watch was written up in full. The traffic was right, so far as I could carry it in my head. It showed me relieved by Lowe at six. It said, ‘Rats, 1;’ and there had been one that night, a large one, which sat for the greater part of an hour on the beam above the Rodriguez recorder, and which I should have known again.

On the third morning I wrote up the watch myself, and then sat down before the book and did not leave it, and Mackellar sat with me for part of the time. Nothing happened to it while we sat there. I do not say that I never took my eyes off the page; a man must light his pipe, and answer when he is spoken to. I can only say that at seven o’clock the writing was mine, and at eight it was mine, and that at noon, when the book had been an hour by itself upon the desk, it was his.

After that I made no more experiments, and Mackellar gave orders that nothing was to be said off the island, and nothing reported to London until we had something that would go upon a service form. I continued to write up my watch each morning, since the regulations require it, and it continued to be done over again for me.

The mess had its own opinion, and it was the old one—that something had happened to Hollis. There is not a ship’s company afloat, nor I think a cable station either, that does not hold that when a man is seen where he cannot be, he is dead or dying; and though no one had seen Hollis, it was felt to come to the same thing. I will own that I thought so myself. We reckoned up among ourselves where he should be by this time: Colombo, we made it, or a day or two beyond—and without anything being said, we waited for bad news.

I will not pretend that I found those watches comfortable. The instrument-room at Cocos at three o’clock in the morning is a quiet place. There are the lamps, and the recorders, and the surf upon the barrier, which never ceases, and which after the first week one does not hear; and there are the rats going about overhead. But I wish to be quite plain upon one point, because some of the versions that I have heard make me see Hollis sitting at the desk. I saw nothing of the kind. I did not at any time see, or hear, or feel anything in that room which was not always there. There was nothing out of the common on that island except the writing in the book.

On the morning of the 30th, at fourteen minutes past two, Batavia called us with a service message from London. I took it myself. It ran, as nearly as I can give it: ‘Regret inform you J. Hollis senior clerk died at sea 22nd instant on board P. and O. homeward mail two days out from Colombo heart failure buried at sea inform staff.’

I sat for a little with the slip in my hand. Then I did what is laid down, and sent the boy across for the Superintendent. Mackellar came over in his sleeping-suit, and read it under the lamp, and said, ‘The 22nd.’ I knew what was in his mind, for it was in mine.

Hollis had died on the 22nd. On the 4th of the month, then, and on the 5th, and on every night up to the 21st, he had been alive, and by all that we afterwards heard in his usual health, on board one steamer or another, or in an hotel at Singapore; and on every one of those nights his hand had kept the middle watch at Cocos. And on the 23rd, and for six nights after that, he had been dead, and under the Arabian Sea; and it had kept it just the same. Whatever it was that we had in the diary, it was not what the mess had supposed. So far as we could see, his dying had made no difference to it, one way or the other.

At six o’clock I wrote up my watch. I entered the service message, with the time of it, and that the Superintendent had been informed; then the barometer and the wind; and I put my initials. I entered no rats, for I had not counted them. I left the book upon the desk. At eight it was in my hand, and at noon it was in my hand; and it is so still, for I looked not a month ago. From that morning the middle watch at Cocos has been in the writing of the man who kept it.

Mackellar wrote across the margin of the 4th of May, in red ink: ‘Middle watch, 4th to 29th inst., entered in the hand of Mr J. Hollis, who left the station on the 3rd and died at sea on the 22nd. Cause not ascertained.—A. M.’ He reported the whole matter to London by the next mail. London asked, by service message, whether the traffic had been correctly handled during the period in question; and upon being told that it had, took no further notice, beyond desiring that the practice of entering vermin in the office diary should be discontinued; which, as will be understood, it had been.

I have often been asked what I think. I do not know that I am obliged to think anything; but as I have been at the pains of writing this down, I will say how it appears to a man of my trade, and it may be taken for what it is worth. I do not believe that Hollis came back. I do not think that he came anywhere. He was on the high seas, thinking, I have no doubt, of Exeter. But a long cable is not like a bell-wire. When the clerk at one end of it lifts his hand from the key, the line does not fall dead upon the instant; it has taken a charge, and it gives the charge back by degrees, and the distant station will see its ink move for a little after the sender has got up and gone to his dinner. Hollis had kept that watch, in that room, for nine years, without missing above a dozen nights. I believe that the watch went on being kept. As to why it should have ceased when we were told of his death, and not when he died, I cannot say; unless it be that the island had till then had no notice of him, and went on working to a man who had closed down. But I do not put this forward as an explanation; I have said that I have none.

I keep the middle watch still, and I enter the barometer and the wind. I have fallen into the way of counting the rats, though I do not enter them. And when my own time comes to go home, I think that I shall ask the man who takes my place to look into the book now and then for the first month or so, and to send me a line.
