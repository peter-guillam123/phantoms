## Life

In 1876, when Hester Charlton was 14, her father drove her out along the Roman Wall to Carrawburgh to see what the landowner’s men were lifting out of a spring. He was a Hexham solicitor and a keen member of the Newcastle antiquaries, and this was the find of his lifetime: a shrine to a goddess nobody had heard of, called Coventina. In the basin were more than 13,000 coins, ten altars, incense burners, and a litter of small things thrown in over some three centuries by people who wanted something. Country people were carrying coins off by the pocketful. Her father’s friends argued about whether it was a temple or a panic. What she took from it was simpler. For three hundred years people had paid the water, and then they had stopped.

She went up to Newnham College in 1881, the year Cambridge first allowed women to sit its honours examinations as of right, and read classics. Cambridge gave her a certificate and no degree. She taught in a girls’ high school for five years and in 1889 joined the staff of the new Royal Holloway College at Egham, where she stayed until she retired. She spent the session of 1893–94 in Greece, among the first few women admitted to the British School at Athens, who were not permitted near an excavation. In 1905 she crossed to Dublin with several hundred other Oxford and Cambridge women and came home with a Trinity College MA. She called it her steamboat degree.

As a scholar she was careful and minor: a handful of notes on Athenian funeral vases and on the lead curse tablets that the Greeks and Romans buried in graves. The stories began as something to read aloud to the college literary society on winter evenings. A colleague sent one to *The Cornhill* without asking her.

## What a ghost was to her

Her dead are older than the gods, and they are owed. The ancients, she thought, had understood this and had forms for it: the offering poured, the days set aside, the door opened and then shut again with the right words. At Athens, when the yearly festival of the dead was over, the householder said: “Out of the doors, ye Keres; it is no longer Anthesteria.” We have kept our dead, she wrote to a friend, and mislaid the receipt.

So a haunting, in a Charlton story, is an unpaid debt, and it is not a moral matter. The dead do not care whether you are good. They want water, or a coin, or a name said aloud. There is a second danger, peculiar to her: scholarship itself. To read an inscription out is to do what it says.

Two things complicate this. She came from a household where the servants talked for a year about Mary Ann Cotton, the County Durham poisoner hanged in 1873, and were not shocked, only interested. She distrusted comfort ever after. And there is envy in her. A woman who did the work for a degree and was not given one knew something about dues withheld, though she would have laughed at the comparison. The ancients at least knew what was owed and to whom. Her modern characters do not, and pay the wrong thing, or pay late.

## The work

Her narrators are scholars, often men, whose authority she borrowed. The prose is measured and exact, with measurements, a Greek phrase glossed in passing, and a dry joke held back until late. The ghost is felt in the body before it is seen: thirst, cold water, a smell of oil. The stories run long, 5,000 to 7,000 words, and end calmly, with the debt paid at a cost.

> The tablet is of lead, four inches by rather less than three, and had been folded twice and pierced with a nail, in the usual way. I say “the usual way” because it is as well to be clear from the outset that there was nothing remarkable about it. Some hundreds of such are known. They ask, for the most part, that a rival’s tongue may be bound, or a thief’s bowels; and they are addressed, as letters must be, to those in a position to oblige. What was unusual in our tablet was only that it had been answered.
^ From “A Leaden Tablet”, *The Cornhill Magazine*, 1899

## Chambers’s Journal

[George Ormiston](../../editor/) read “A Leaden Tablet” and wrote to “H. C.” care of *The Cornhill*. She replied on college paper, agreed, and asked him not to print her name. “The Well at the Mile-Castle” (1903), in which the workmen clearing a Roman well keep throwing their own pennies into it, was the first of four stories she gave him. When Jane Harrison’s celebrated book on Greek religion appeared that same year, full of ghosts and their placation, Charlton remarked to Ormiston that she had been saying as much in fiction since 1896, and had been paid rather less.
