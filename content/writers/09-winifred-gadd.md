## Life

Winifred Gadd’s father was a shipping clerk, and in 1904 he moved his family from East Ham to Ilford, to one of the thousands of new houses built there for men like him: a bay window, a strip of garden and a fast train to Liverpool Street. Ilford had 11,000 people in 1890 and 78,000 twenty years later. She left school at 15, learned shorthand and typing, and went up to the City each morning with half the young women of the district.

In January 1917 the munitions works at Silvertown, five miles away, blew up and killed 73 people. She watched the sky go red from the back bedroom. In April her brother Leonard, two years older, was posted missing in France. There was no grave, and for a long time no certainty. By then she had left the City for the order office of the plate works in the town, and she spent the rest of the war typing invoices for the glass negatives on which the country was photographing its soldiers.

In 1919 her mother began to attend a spiritualist circle in Forest Gate, and Winifred went with her. The medium brought messages from “Len”. Her mother was comforted. Winifred, who knew from her work exactly how two faces are got onto one plate, watched a stranger feed her mother the thing she was starving for, and could not decide which of the three of them she was angriest with.

She started writing in 1921. In October 1922 Percy Thompson was stabbed to death in Belgrave Road, ten minutes’ walk from the Gadds’ house. His wife Edith was 28, a City businesswoman who caught the same trains. She had written her lover more than sixty letters, full of novels and daydreams. They were read out in court as evidence that she had urged him to it, and she was hanged on 9 January 1923. The two women never spoke. Gadd said later that it was the first time she understood what could be done with a woman’s imaginings once somebody had typed and numbered them.

She did not marry. She wrote for ten years, stopped when the magazines thinned out, and worked for the plate company until 1954.

## What a ghost was to her

She began from disbelief. The dead do not come. The living send for them, out of a need they cannot stand, and something answers to the name. In the Forest Gate circle the something was a woman in a cardigan. That was the easy case.

The stories are about the hard one, where there is no medium to blame. A girl alone in an office wants, without quite admitting it, to hear a particular voice, and the telephone rings. Gadd could never settle whether the thing that answers is inside the person wanting or outside her. Freud’s ideas were just then reaching the Sunday papers, and she had read enough to know that a wish can act without its owner’s leave. She had not read enough to be reassured.

There is a second fear, and it is the Thompson case. Her heroines are frightened of the apparition, but they are more frightened of their own minds being produced as exhibits. Each of her ghosts comes through a machine she herself operated for a living, a typewriter, a switchboard or a photographic plate, and each leaves a record. She signed herself “W. Gadd” so that editors would take her for a man, and burned her photographs before she died.

## The work

The stories are short, 1,500 to 3,000 words, quick and modern. The setting is the office, the train, the teashop, the bed-sitting-room with a gas ring. Thought runs straight into action. The ghost could always be the heroine’s own doing. She stops in the middle of a gesture and gives no answer.

> After six the switchboard was nobody’s, and Doris, who stayed late on Thursdays to finish the foreign post, had been told to let it ring. It rang at ten past. She let it. It rang again at the quarter, on the same line, and she watched the little shutter drop and thought, quite distinctly, that it was for her. That was nonsense, because nobody she knew had the number, and nobody she had known was on the telephone at all. She put the plug in. “There you are,” said a man’s voice, close, and rather pleased.
^ From “After Six”, printed in a London evening paper, 1923

## Chambers’s Journal

She sent a story to Edinburgh in 1924. [George Ormiston](../../editor/), then 62 and five years from retirement, bought it and asked for another, in a letter that began “Dear Mr. Gadd”. She never corrected him. “An Unexposed Plate” (1925) concerns a customer’s complaint to a plate works: a face on a plate that he swears was never in a camera. The clerk who opens the letter knows the face, and its owner is alive. She was the last writer Ormiston found, and the only one of the ten he never knew to be a woman.
