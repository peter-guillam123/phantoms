## Life

Mossley grew up across the meeting point of three counties. Eli Kenworthy was born in the part that lay in Saddleworth, and so in Yorkshire, and worked all his life a few hundred yards away in Lancashire. He was four when the war in America stopped the cotton coming, and eight when it came back. In between, his father was out of work and the family lived on relief. Two miles down the valley, at Stalybridge, the hussars were called out in March 1863 against people who had rioted when the relief was cut.

At eight he went into the mill as a half-timer: mornings among the spinning mules as a little piecer, afternoons at school. At 13 he was there all day. He became a minder, in charge of a pair of mules, and remained one. He learned to read properly at a night class, and read what was on the shelves: Bunyan, Burns, Dickens, and the Lancashire dialect men, Edwin Waugh and Ben Brierley. Samuel Laycock, the Stalybridge weaver, had written songs about the Cotton Famine, and Eli’s mother sang them.

In 1886 the new *Cotton Factory Times*, a penny weekly for mill workers, printed a sketch of his. He wrote for it, and for papers like it, until 1920. The name he signed, “Eli o’ Betty’s”, is simply Eli, Betty’s son, which is how people were told apart in a valley with a dozen surnames to go round. He went to chapel, played in the band, and never lived more than a mile from where he was born.

## What a ghost was to him

Two murders hung over his district, 77 years apart, and nobody was hanged for either. In 1832 an innkeeper of 84 and his son were beaten to death at a lonely public house on the moor road above Greenfield. The case is known by the family’s by-name, Bill o’ Jack’s. Thousands walked up to look, Eli’s grandmother among them. In 1909 the mill owner George Harry Storrs was stabbed in his own house at Stalybridge; two men were tried, one after the other, and both were acquitted. Kenworthy drew the obvious conclusion about how much to expect from a court.

So his ghosts are creditors. They come back because somebody did them out of something and there was nowhere to take the complaint. A boggart, he said, is the poor man’s magistrate.

But notice the size of the claims. It is always sixpence on the rent, a week’s wage wrongly stopped, a good name. Kenworthy was no firebrand. He was a chapel man who kept his job for sixty years, and his boggarts do on his behalf what he would never have done himself, and no more. They are also funny, and they are company. The old boggart tales of the district were comic as often as not, and he held that a story should make people laugh before it makes them go quiet. Asked whether he believed any of it, he said: “I never seed nowt mysel’, but I’ve known them as did, an’ they weren’t liars.”

## The work

The tales are short, 1,500 to 3,000 words, and told by a man with listeners, in a taproom or an engine house. The narration is plain English with a northern turn to it. The talk is in dialect, kept readable. The ghost is heard before it is seen: clogs on the setts, a cough, coals shifted in a cellar. The master or his agent gets what is coming, and the teller has the last dry word.

> Owd Jonty Schofield allus said he’d no objection to a boggart as paid its road. “Ther’s one i’ our cellar,” he’d tell folk, “an’ it’s theer rent free, which is moor nor I am.” It had come wi’ th’ house, like th’ damp. It did no harm beyond shiftin’ th’ coals about of a neet, and Jonty’s wife reckoned it were company. It were only when th’ maister’s agent come up from Stalybridge to put th’ rent up sixpence that it took agen onybody; an’ it took agen him.
^ From “Jonty’s Lodger”, *Cotton Factory Times*, 1894

## Chambers’s Journal

[George Ormiston](../../editor/) came across a Kenworthy sketch reprinted in a Manchester weekly and wrote to him care of the paper. The reply, which Ormiston kept, is in pencil on a page torn from a rent book. Ormiston asked for a story with the telling in plain English and the talk left alone. “The Little Piecer” (1905) is about a boy killed under a mule who is still seen cleaning there each breakfast time, until the week the firm pays his mother the wages it had stopped. It was the only time Kenworthy was printed outside Lancashire, and he was paid more for it than for a year of sketches.
