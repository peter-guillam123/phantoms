## Life

Harriet Tugwell was eight in the summer of 1860, when a boy of three was taken from his cot at Road Hill House and found in the garden privy with his throat cut. The house stood two miles from her father’s vicarage. Nobody told her what had happened. She worked it out, she wrote much later, from the way her own house changed: the nursery door locked at night, a new bolt on the back stairs, the maids falling silent when she came into a room.

The detective sent from London suspected the dead boy’s half-sister, Constance Kent, who was 16. She was arrested and let go. For five years the neighbourhood lived with a murder it believed had been done by someone inside that family, and went on paying calls. In 1865 Constance confessed. People who had said for five years that they always knew found they had nothing to add.

Harriet was taught at home, with one year at a school in Bath. Her father died in 1879, and a vicarage goes with the living. She and her mother had three months to leave, and took rooms in Frome. It was there that she began to write, for the money. Her mother died in 1883. The next year Harriet went to keep house for her younger brother Edmund, a priest vicar of Wells Cathedral, in one of the fourteenth-century houses of Vicars’ Close, and she lived there until she died.

She wrote in the mornings, called it “my scribbling”, and seems to have earned about £30 in a good year. The magazines that bought her closed one after another: *Belgravia* in 1899, *The Argosy* in 1901, *Longman’s* in 1905, *Temple Bar* in 1906. She did not try the new ones, which she thought noisy, and published nothing after that.

## What a ghost was to her

She was a plain, orthodox Anglican. The dead were with God, and it would have been a kind of impertinence to suppose them wandering about Somerset. So nothing in her stories comes back. What haunts her houses is a thing the people in them have agreed not to mention, and the haunting is done by the living to each other. It lives in arrangements: the room that is not used, the name not given to a child, the subject changed.

It would be easy to read this as an accusation, and she is sharper about families than her manner suggests. But she is not simply on the side of speaking out. She had seen what the alternative looked like in 1865, when a girl’s confession was dragged through every newspaper in the country, and she knew that the silence she describes is also what holds a household up. In her best stories the outsider who understands what is wrong does not expose it. She packs, and leaves, and the house goes on. Whether that is cowardice or tact the stories decline to say.

## The work

The stories are long for their kind, 4,000 to 6,000 words, and told from close beside someone with no power in the house: a governess, a companion, a curate’s new wife. The sentences are balanced and dry. Almost nothing is seen. There is a scent of lavender water, a door always found shut, a second cup. The servants know. She ends on a small domestic act, and never on an explanation.

> It was understood at Combe Leigh that the morning-room was not used in winter. Nobody had told Miss Fenn so; it had been conveyed to her, in her first week, by the way Mrs. Pargeter’s hand rested for a moment on the door as they passed it, and by the housemaid, who dusted there on Thursdays with the door propped open by a chair. In March, when the fires were let out elsewhere, she observed that it was not used in spring either.
^ From “The Morning-Room”, *Temple Bar*, 1891

## Chambers’s Journal

When *Belgravia* closed in 1899, [George Ormiston](../../editor/) of *Chambers’s Journal* wrote to her through the publisher of *Temple Bar*, asking for “one of your houses”. He had read her for years without knowing her name. She sent “The Reader” (1900), in which a paid companion reads aloud each evening to an old lady, and is corrected each evening for leaving out a passage that is not in the book. She wrote twice more for him and no further.
