## Life

John Trembath’s father was a mine captain, which in Cornwall means the man in charge of the work underground. By the 1870s the tin was failing, and half of St Just had gone to the mines of America, Australia and the Cape. The father looked at the price of tin and put his son to “the Cable”.

The cable came ashore at Porthcurno, eight miles off, a cove under the cliffs where the telegraph to India had landed since 1870. Trembath went there at 16 to learn to send and to read. A cable signal is a faint thing. At first it was a spot of light trembling on a scale; later an ink line wavering down a paper tape, called the slip. An operator learns to tell each distant sender by his touch, as one knows a voice.

The Eastern Telegraph Company then sent him abroad and kept him there: Gibraltar, Aden, Zanzibar; from 1900 Ascension Island, a cinder in the South Atlantic to which the cable had come the year before; later the Cocos Islands in the Indian Ocean. A station was a dozen men, a mess, a billiard table and a boat every few weeks. He left Cocos two years before November 1914, when a landing party from the German cruiser *Emden* smashed its instruments, and he knew most of the men who were lined up to watch.

He was at home on leave in January 1893 when the miners at Wheal Owles, in St Just, broke through into the flooded workings of an old mine alongside. Nineteen men and a boy drowned in the dark. The bodies were never recovered, and the mine was abandoned over them. He had been at school with some.

He retired to Penzance in 1921.

## What a ghost was to him

Trembath did not believe that the dead return. He believed something stranger, and held it calmly. A message, he knew from his work, is only a small movement, read by a tired man at night. It can go on travelling after the hand that sent it has stopped. Cables carry things nobody sent: earth currents, strays, the line’s own noise. A ghost is traffic of that kind. It is a signal with no sender, or with a sender who is no longer there to stop sending.

He had the idea from two places. One was the instrument room. The other was his grandmother, who told him about the knockers, the small people who tap in the rock ahead of the miners. You listen, and then you decide what it means. West Cornwall also held that the drowned could be heard at night, hailing their own names from the sea. To him it was all one thing. The tapping is neutral. The terrible part is that a trained man cannot help reading it.

So the fear in his stories is of a message addressed to nobody, or, worse, of one addressed to you by name from a station that is not sending. What makes him unusual is that he seems to have found this steadying. He had been raised a Methodist and had let it go. The world, as he came to see it, was very large, mostly water and wire, and not much interested in us; and nineteen men and a boy were still under St Just. He does not rage at that, and he does not look away from it. He logs it.

## The work

He writes as an operator making a report: first person, modest, methodical, with the times given. There is shop talk, lightly worn, and very little dialogue. The ghost is a pattern, a touch on the line that he does not know, or knows and should not be hearing. He ends by going off watch. The last line is often the entry in the station diary. The stories run from 3,000 to 5,000 words.

> The night traffic at Ascension is nothing to speak of. After St. Helena has cleared, at about two, a man may sit an hour with the recorder drawing a straight line down the slip and nothing to do but watch it and keep awake. I mention this to show that I was not busy and not, I think, careless. At 2.40 the line moved. It was not St. Helena; I knew every hand there as I know my brother’s. It sent slowly, like a learner, and what it sent was the call for Porthcurno, and after it my own name.
^ From “The Slow Hand”, *The Zodiac*, the Eastern Telegraph Company’s staff magazine, 1908

## Chambers’s Journal

*Chambers’s Journal* liked factual articles from men in odd corners of the world, and in 1910 Trembath sent it one on life at a cable station. [George Ormiston](../../editor/) printed it and asked, in a postscript, whether anything had ever happened out there that he could not account for. The answer was “The Diary at Direction Island” (1911): a relief operator arrives to find the station diary written up to that day, in the hand of a man who left on the last boat. Trembath wrote for Ormiston until Ormiston retired.
