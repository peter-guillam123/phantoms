## Life

East Garston lies in the valley of the Lambourn, under the downs. A few miles off are the White Horse and the old tomb called Wayland’s Smithy, where a traveller was told to leave his horse and a coin and come back to find it shod. Thomas Lovegrove drove a plough team there at 12. At 19 he walked to Reading and enlisted in the 66th Regiment at the barracks then going up on the Oxford Road. He was sent out to India with a draft.

On 27 July 1880 the 66th went into action at Maiwand, in Afghanistan. It lost 286 dead, more than six in every ten men present, and both its colours. Lovegrove was not among the eleven who made the last stand in a walled garden at Khig. None of them lived, and the story reached the British months later from an Afghan officer. He was in the retreat, some 45 miles to Kandahar without water. When the survivors came in he was one of those who called the roll.

He stayed in the Army, and it made him. It taught him to write a fair hand and keep books, and he had a head for stores. He rose by every step there was, corporal to quartermaster-sergeant, and in 1898 was commissioned Quartermaster with the honorary rank of lieutenant, which was where a man from the ranks stopped. He served through the South African war and retired in 1906 to a terrace off the Oxford Road, with his wife Ellen, who had packed biscuits at Huntley & Palmers, and two daughters.

In 1896 he was on the depot staff at Reading. That spring the bodies of infants were taken from the Thames in parcels, and Amelia Dyer was hanged for it in June. In July a trooper of the Royal Horse Guards, Charles Wooldridge, was hanged in Reading Gaol for killing his wife. Wooldridge was an East Garston boy, six years the younger. Lovegrove had known the family.

## What a ghost was to him

He passed the Maiwand Lion in Forbury Gardens most days of his retirement. It carries the names of 329 officers and men, and he had known a good many of them.

His idea of a ghost is the simplest of the ten. The dead man is still on the strength. Nobody relieved him, or nobody struck his name off, and so he carries on. There is no malice in it. What is wanted is the proper word from the proper authority, and when it is given the man goes. Lovegrove believed this flatly, as a thing he had seen, and would not argue it.

What he never says is why the fright in his stories is always a matter of arithmetic. A roll answers one over. A ration is drawn for a man who is not there. He had been counted in, at Kandahar, when 286 others were not, and for forty years he handled the returns that record who is present and who is not. The stories keep putting the numbers right. They read as comfort, and they are also the nearest thing to a confession that a man of his kind would make.

## The work

He writes as an old soldier setting down what happened, in order. The sentences are short. Numbers, times and distances are exact, and army terms are used correctly and not explained. He has no adjectives for fear. Officers are seen from below, with affection. The ghost is seen in daylight, correctly dressed. The ending is an entry in a book.

> We paraded ninety-one rifles on the morning of the second day, and I know that because I called the roll myself, the colour-sergeant being dead. Ninety-one answered. I marked the state accordingly and took it to Mr. Harding, who signed it without reading it, as officers do. It was not until the evening, when I came to draw water for the company, that I found I had drawn for ninety-two, and that none of it was left over.
^ From “The Parade State”, first printed in his regimental journal, 1906

## Chambers’s Journal

“The Parade State” was sent to Edinburgh by a retired colonel who wrote for *Chambers’s* on military subjects. [George Ormiston](../../editor/) reprinted it in 1907 and asked for another. Lovegrove sent “The Standing Order” (1908), about a sentry posted on an empty store and relieved every two hours since 1857, because nobody had cancelled the order. He wrote for the *Journal* for the rest of his life, and kept every one of Ormiston’s letters.
