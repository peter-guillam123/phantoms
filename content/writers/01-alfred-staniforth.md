## Life

Alfred Staniforth was six years old on the night of 11 March 1864, when the new dam at Dale Dyke gave way above Sheffield and the water came down the Loxley and the Don. At least 240 people died. His family, in Neepsend, did not. What stayed with him, by his own account, was the weeks afterwards: a tide line on the wallpaper, and his mother at the table listing what was gone, with a price beside each thing, for the water company’s claim.

His father cut files by hand, a trade that slowly poisoned its men with the lead they worked on. Alfred left school at 12 and was bound to the *Sheffield Independent* as a compositor’s apprentice, which was then a common way into reporting. He taught himself shorthand and by 18 was covering police courts and inquests. That was 1876, the year Charles Peace shot Arthur Dyson at Banner Cross. For two years and three months Peace was the best story in Sheffield: the hunt, the arrest in Blackheath under another name, the leap from the train taking him north, the trial at Leeds, the hanging at Armley. Staniforth wrote some of it and read all of it.

He moved to the paper’s evening sister, and when a rival swallowed it he went to London, early in 1888, and sold paragraphs by the line. One buyer was T. P. O’Connor’s new halfpenny *Star*. He was working the East End that autumn when the *Star* made a bogeyman called “Leather Apron” out of street talk and sold more copies than it ever had.

A staff job followed, on the sub-editors’ table of an evening paper, and a wife, Ada, a compositor’s daughter from Walworth, and three children. From 1892 he wrote stories on Sundays because the monthlies paid better than his paper did. He stopped in 1914. There is no sign that he ever met another author.

## What a ghost was to him

He called it a trade, like the cutlery trade, and said so to anyone who asked. A ghost was a device for producing one fright, properly prepared and plainly shown. He believed in nothing, went to no church, and thought the literary ghost story was fog, written by people who could not get a body through a door.

That is the stated position, and the stories mostly bear it out. But read thirty of them together and two habits show that he does not seem to have known he had. His ghosts are never met alone. There is always a crowd: passengers, a queue, a jury, people at windows. And somebody always makes a list. The effects of the deceased are itemised, with values. The Sheffield lesson, that sales were highest while Peace was missing, is in every plot. So, less comfortably, is the child at the table, watching a catastrophe turned into an inventory. Fear, for Staniforth, is a public event, and it has a price.

## The work

The stories run to 4,000 words at most and are told by a newspaper man, straight to the reader, in the easy, knowing manner of the comic papers he also wrote for. He begins by telling you how such things are faked, because he has faked them. Then he gives the facts as a reporter would: a time, a street, an occupation, a price. The ghost is seen, solid, and described as a wanted man would be. The last line is a sting, and it is often a sum of money. The manner depends on one effect, which is that the man who jokes about everything stops joking.

> At ten minutes past eleven on the night of the fourteenth of November, the last car but one left the Nether Edge terminus with three passengers inside and none on top. The conductor, a steady man named Hobson with eleven years’ service, was prepared to swear to this before any coroner in England, and in the event he had to. For when the car reached Moorhead there were four inside; and the fourth had not paid.
^ From “The Last Car but One”, *Pearson’s Magazine*, 1899

## Chambers’s Journal

Staniforth sent two stories to Edinburgh in 1901 and had both back. With the second came a note from the assistant editor, [George Ormiston](../../editor/), asking for one “with fewer corpses and one good idea”. The result, “A Slack Week in August”, in which a reporter invents a haunting to fill a column and receives letters confirming it before the column is printed, ran in 1902. It is the nearest he came to writing about himself.
