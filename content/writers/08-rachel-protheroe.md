## Life

Rachel Protheroe was a year old when Sarah Jacob died, two farms away. Sarah was the girl who was said to live without food. For two years people came to the farm at Lletherneuadd to see her lying in bed, and her fame reached the London medical press. In December 1869 four nurses were sent down from Guy’s Hospital to watch that she ate nothing. They watched, nobody fed her, and in eight days she was dead. She was twelve. Her father and mother went to prison. Rachel’s mother had been one of the visitors, and never spoke of it afterwards without lowering her voice.

Welsh was the language of the house, the chapel and the Sunday school, where she learned to read. English was the language of the day school. She was clever, and at 13 the master kept her on as a pupil-teacher for a few pounds a year. She won a place at the training college for women at Swansea, then the only one in Wales, and came home in 1889 with her certificate. She taught infants, and later had a small school of her own, in the wool villages below Llandysul. A mistress who married lost her post. She did not marry.

As a girl she had read the magazine that the poet Cranogwen edited for the women of Wales, and she wanted to be in print. She chose to write in English, and her neighbours were not sure they forgave her. Her material was what the children’s grandmothers told her, and she wrote it down exactly.

She was 36 when the religious revival of 1904 came out of the chapels a few miles from her door. She sat through its meetings, by her own account, half carried away and half watching. Many of the boys she taught went east to the coal. After the explosion at Senghenydd in October 1913, which killed 439 men, she read the lists for names from her own registers, and found some.

## What a ghost was to her

In the tradition she grew up in, the uncanny comes before a death and not after it. The *canwyll corff*, the corpse candle, is a small light that leaves the house and travels to the churchyard by the road the funeral will take. The *toili* is the funeral itself, met on the lane before anybody has died. The *tolaeth* is a sound: the carpenter hears a coffin being made in his empty workshop. The old explanation was kindly. St David, it was said, had prayed that his people might have warning of death, so as to be ready.

In a Protheroe story nobody gets ready. The light is seen, and then there is a week to be lived through. The fear is of knowing: whose it will be, whether to tell, and that nothing anyone does will turn the light off its road. She had been raised in a chapel that taught that what will happen is already settled, and the omens fit that teaching as a key fits a lock.

There is a harder thing under it, which goes back to Lletherneuadd. Her stories are full of people who watch and wait and say nothing: at doors, at windows, across a lane. A whole district had once watched a child, and waited. She never wrote about Sarah Jacob. She did not need to. Asked if she had seen the candle herself, she said: “No. My mother did.”

## The work

The tales are plain and short, 2,000 to 3,500 words. The sentences are joined by “and”, in the cadence of the Welsh Bible. People are named by their farms. The dialogue is spare English in Welsh order. The ghost never has a face: it is a light, a sound, a procession seen from behind. The story ends when the thing foreseen happens, in one line, or just before.

> On the Tuesday before Michaelmas, Hannah Penrallt saw a light go down the lane from Blaencwm. It was a small light and it did not hurry. It went the way a person would go who was carrying it, round by the gate and not through the hedge, and at the ford it stopped a moment as if for the stones. She stood at her door and watched it up the far bank and along to the chapel, and in at the burying-ground. Then she went in and banked the fire. There were three at Blaencwm, and she did not know which.
^ From “The Light from Blaencwm”, *Wales*, 1896

## Chambers’s Journal

In 1908 she sent *Chambers’s Journal* a factual article on death omens in the Teifi valley. [George Ormiston](../../editor/) printed it, and wrote to ask whether she could make a story of one of them. “Tolaeth” (1909) is about a carpenter who hears a coffin made in his shop at night and, being a craftsman, can tell the length of it by ear. Ormiston took a story from her most years after that. He once asked her to add a paragraph explaining what was going on. She declined, and he printed it as it stood.
