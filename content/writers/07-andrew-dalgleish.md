## Life

Andrew Dalgleish’s father was one of the ministers who walked out of the Church of Scotland in 1843, giving up church, manse and stipend to found the Free Church. The son was raised on the catechism and on its hardest doctrine: that some are chosen and some are not, that nothing a man does will alter which he is, and that an account is kept.

He went to the Royal High School and at 17 into an Edinburgh law office. He qualified in 1879 as a Solicitor before the Supreme Courts, a respectable body one step below the Writers to the Signet in grandeur, and conscious of the step. His work was the dull kind: property, and the winding up of dead men’s affairs. In his last year as an apprentice the City of Glasgow Bank failed and ruined its shareholders, and he spent months making lists of the furniture of widows.

His pleasure was the criminal courts. He sat on the public benches for Eugène Chantrelle, the French teacher hanged in 1878 for poisoning his wife, and for Jessie King, the Stockbridge baby farmer, in 1889. At night he read old trials. The one he returned to was a Highland case of 1754. Two men were tried in Edinburgh for the murder, five years before, of an English sergeant named Davies. The principal evidence came from a shepherd, who said that the sergeant’s ghost had come to him and told him where the bones lay. Counsel for the defence asked what language the ghost had spoken. Gaelic, said the shepherd. Pretty well, said counsel, for the ghost of an English sergeant. The jury acquitted.

He never married. A sister kept house for him in Newington. He was an elder of his church until about 1900, and then stopped attending, without explanation.

## What a ghost was to him

A ghost, to Dalgleish, is evidence. He does not ask whether it is real. He asks what it proves, who stands to gain, and whether it would survive five minutes of questioning. He thought the Scottish verdict of Not Proven the only honest finding available on most of life, and his stories end on it.

That is the lawyer. The son of the manse is underneath, and is the more interesting. What unsettles Dalgleish is never the dead. It is the record: the idea, drilled into him before he could read, that a complete and accurate account of a man exists somewhere and may one day be produced. His worst apparitions are of the living. A man meets his own signature on a deed he never saw, or learns that he was seen, by a reliable witness, somewhere he has never been. Scotland had a word for this, the co-walker, and a long literature of doubles from Hogg to Stevenson. Dalgleish gave it a filing system.

It may explain why he leaves every case open. A verdict would have to be a verdict on someone, and he preferred not to say on whom.

## The work

A Dalgleish story is a file. Witness statements, letters and inventories are laid out and joined by a cool commentary in a lawyer’s voice. Scots law terms are used correctly. The witnesses speak Scots and the narrator does not. Nothing supernatural is ever shown. The fright is in a discrepancy: a date, a signature, a train that did not run. They run to 4,000 or 6,000 words.

> I have before me the precognition of Janet Wauchope or Hislop, widow, residing at Easter Howgate, taken by the Procurator Fiscal on the ninth of March. It runs to four pages and is, as these things go, a careful piece of work. The witness states that she saw the deceased at the stair-foot at or about six in the evening; that he wore his grey topcoat; and that he bade her good-night. So far there is nothing to detain us. The difficulty, which the Fiscal has noted in the margin in pencil, is that she is speaking of the tenth.
^ From “The Tenth of March”, *Blackwood’s Magazine*, 1894, unsigned. A precognition is a witness’s statement taken before trial.

## Chambers’s Journal

Dalgleish and [George Ormiston](../../editor/) were Edinburgh men of nearly the same age and lunched together for thirty years. Ormiston asked him for a Christmas story in 1897, his first winter as assistant editor, and got “A Looking-Glass, of No Value”: an inventory of a dead man’s goods in which one item is refused, in turn, by everyone it is left to. It was the first story Ormiston commissioned from anyone. Dalgleish is also the source, through his letters, of most of what is known about Ormiston.
