## Life

Nora Tobin’s father kept a drapery in Clonmel and gave credit to half the county. He had been a boy during the Famine, when grain went down to the quay for export under guard and the town saw food riots. He rarely spoke of it. He never, his daughter said, refused tea and candles to a house with a death in it.

She boarded with the Ursuline nuns at Thurles from 12 to 17 and came home with French, some Italian, a good hand and the piano. Then she went into the shop to keep the books. One of the town’s dressmakers at that time had an apprentice, a quick, well-dressed girl of 18 from the country near Fethard, who was sent in for thread and trimmings. Her name was Bridget Boland. In August 1887 she married a Clonmel cooper called Michael Cleary. Nora had served her a dozen times and thought no more about her.

She began sending verses to *The Irish Monthly* in 1888. Its editor, the Jesuit Matthew Russell, coaxed stories out of her instead, and she wrote about the people who came into the shop.

In March 1895 Bridget Cleary fell ill at home in Ballyvadlea. Her husband became convinced that the woman in the bed was not his wife but a changeling left by the fairies. With her father, her aunt and her cousins helping or looking on, he forced herbs into her, held her over the kitchen fire, and on the night of the 15th burned her to death. The trial was held that July at Clonmel courthouse, a short walk from the Tobins’ shop. London papers called it witch-burning and asked what such a people would do with Home Rule.

Nora Tobin was 29. She married Daniel Heffernan, a national schoolteacher, in 1898, had three children, and kept her own name on her work. The war of independence began twenty miles away, at Soloheadbeg, in January 1919. She wrote her last ghost story the following year, and said afterwards that the country had enough dead to be going on with.

## What a ghost was to her

The dead, in her stories, are neighbours. Most are in purgatory, some are doing their time close to home, and now and then one of them wants a thing done: a Mass said, a debt paid, a message carried. Nobody screams. You answer civilly and do what is asked. This was not a literary idea. It was what her mother believed and what her customers believed, the same people who swept the hearth and left the door on the latch for the dead on All Souls’ night.

The fear is somewhere else. It is in the living, and in what a townland will do once it has decided that a woman is not who she says she is.

After 1895 she was in a difficult place and knew it. She could not mock the old beliefs, because they were bound up with her own faith in the dead and her duty to them. She could not treat them as poetry either, as Mr Yeats and his friends in Dublin were doing, because she had seen where they could end. She had also noticed a date. In 1766 a priest, Nicholas Sheehy, was hanged outside Clonmel gaol on evidence the town never believed. It was 15 March. Her stories walk the line between a prayer for the dead and a woman held over a fire, and they are careful, and funny, and never comfortable.

## The work

She writes as a townswoman who knows everyone’s business. The English is that of an Irish country town, never the stage version, and money is counted to the penny. The ghost is met on the road or at the counter, in its own clothes, and it talks. The stories run to 3,000 or 4,500 words and end either with something done, or with the neighbours.

> My father gave credit to the dead. I do not mean that he was a soft man, for he was not, and there were families in the Commons that got nothing from him but the time of day. I mean only that when Mrs. Dwyer came in on the fair day of November and asked for the half-pound of tea and the candles to be put down to her husband, who was eleven months buried, he took the book from under the counter and wrote it in. “He was always good for it,” my father said, and he was right, for it was paid by Christmas, though not by her.
^ From “The Dwyer Account”, *The Irish Monthly*, 1893

## Chambers’s Journal

Father Russell made a habit of placing his contributors elsewhere, and in 1904 he sent some of her work to Edinburgh. [George Ormiston](../../editor/) asked her for a story and got “Short Weight”, about a soul doing its purgatory at the back door of the shop where, in life, it had cheated on the scales. He asked for more for years. She sent three, and told him she could not write about Tipperary for Scotch Presbyterians more often than that.
