## The Journal

*Chambers’s Journal* began in February 1832 as a penny weekly for people who could not afford books, and outlived almost every magazine founded after it. By the 1890s it was a staid, cheap, widely read family paper, published from a rambling building at 339 High Street on Edinburgh’s Royal Mile. Its editor from 1888 was Charles E. S. Chambers, grandson of one of the founders, who was also chairman of the firm and had the firm to run.

The *Journal* was known for reading what it was sent. In 1883 it reported that 3,225 manuscripts had arrived that year and that 330 had been accepted. Four years earlier one of those acceptances had been a first story by a medical student called Conan Doyle, who was paid three guineas and not named. Somebody had to read the other 2,895.

## Life

From 1884 that somebody was George Ormiston. He was the son of a compositor in the firm’s printing office and had come into the building at 15 as a reader’s boy, holding the copy while the proofreader checked the type against it. He was moved upstairs to the manuscripts at 22. In 1896 he was made assistant editor, a title used inside the house and never printed, and he held it until he retired in 1929.

Very little else is certain. He married; his wife’s name was Agnes; there were no children. He lived in a flat off Leith Walk and later at Portobello, by the sea. He lunched for thirty years with the solicitor [Andrew Dalgleish](../writers/andrew-dalgleish/), whose letters are the main source for his opinions, and who said of him that he had read more bad prose than any man in Scotland and held it against nobody.

## What he wanted

The *Journal* did not much go in for ghost stories. Its readers wanted secret agents, stolen jewels and articles on salmon. Ormiston liked them, and got them in when he could. He seems to have had one test. Dalgleish put it this way: “George will take any ghost that has cost the teller something.” He had no interest in machinery, and sent back anything in which the apparition could have been swapped for a different one without loss. What he looked for was a writer whose ghost could only have been that writer’s: who had got it from somewhere, a place or a trade or a death, and was not quite comfortable handing it over.

That would explain the oddest thing about his list, which is how little the ten have in common. They were born between 1852 and 1894. Two of them left school before they were thirteen and one lectured in Greek. They include a soldier, a spinner and a typist. None of them, as far as can be established, ever met another. Some he found in other magazines and wrote to. Some came in with the post. Most of them wrote chiefly for other people, and several never knew he had a hand in anyone’s work but their own.

## After

He retired in 1929 and died five years later. The firm left the High Street building soon afterwards, and the *Journal* itself lasted until 1956. Ormiston’s name was never printed in it.
