## What this is

Phantoms is going to be a collection of short ghost stories, each written in the voice of a different writer from the years between about 1880 and 1925. The stories are only beginning: there are three so far. Around it are the ten writers, and the editor who published them.

I got the idea from the British Library’s collections of forgotten ghost stories, which dig through old magazines for work by people nobody remembers. Thousands of these stories were printed, most of them by amateurs. I wondered what would happen if you invented ten of those people in enough detail (where they grew up, what they did for a living, which local murder they heard about as children) and then asked each of them to write. Would the stories come out different from one another? Would any of them be good? This site is the experiment.

## What is real and what is not

**The ten writers never existed. Neither did George Ormiston.** Their stories, the titles of those stories and the lines quoted from them are invented too. Nobody called Eli Kenworthy wrote for the *Cotton Factory Times*, and no Harriet Tugwell kept house in Vicars’ Close.

Almost everything around them is real. *Chambers’s Journal* was a real magazine with a real office on the High Street in Edinburgh, and the other magazines named are real too. The Sheffield flood, the murder at Road Hill House, the battle of Maiwand, Coventina’s Well, the burning of Bridget Cleary, the ghost who gave evidence in 1754, the Welsh Fasting Girl, the Wheal Owles disaster and the Thompson and Bywaters case all happened as described, as far as I have been able to check. The “Further reading” links on each page go to real sources about those real things.

**The pictures are made up as well.** They were produced by an AI image maker from written descriptions, in the manner of old photographs and drawings. Nobody in them ever lived, and no such print or sketch exists.

So each page is a made-up life laid carefully over true history. If you find yourself half believing in one of them, that is the idea. But please don’t cite them.

## How it is made

I am not a programmer or a historian. I describe what I want and an AI, Anthropic’s Claude, does the research, the drafting and the typing, and then we argue about it. The research has limits, and I would sooner say so. Much of it was checked against Wikipedia and a few archives and museums, some of it only against search results, and a few details come from the model’s memory and still need confirming. The working notes say which is which.

The pictures were made with ChatGPT’s image maker, from descriptions that Claude wrote. The site is plain web pages, built by a short script. It sets no cookies and tracks nobody. The typeface is Libre Caslon, a free revival of the letter most Victorian printers reached for.

## Diary

**12 November 2026. Faces, or the lack of them.** *Milestone.* Every biography had a caption for a picture that did not exist: a brass band with him somewhere among the horns, a staff portrait where her own hat hides her eyes, a wedding picture lost in a house move. I had Claude write a description of each and gave them to ChatGPT’s image maker. They came back better than I expected, and they are now on the pages. They are fakes of old photographs of people who never lived, and the note above says so. In the one of the cathedral choir, look at the upstairs window on the right.

**12 November 2026. Line by line.** I went through the Staniforth rewrite sentence by sentence and sent notes, and it was worth doing, because the same fault kept turning up in different clothes. A clause would sound as though it was making a point, I would go back to find the point, and there was none. We ended up with a test for it: the shape of a sentence must not promise more weight than its content carries. About a dozen lines changed. I was also argued with, usefully. Strip every such flourish from every writer and they all end up sounding alike, so some stay where they are doing a job.

**12 November 2026. Staniforth, done again.** I reread “The Last Car but One” overnight and it was wrong. It was told as a bare record of an inquest, by a reporter keeping himself out of it, and that left a frightening idea feeling antiseptic. It was also not how the papers of the 1890s wrote, which was personally and with opinions. So it has been rewritten. The man telling it now is a newspaper cynic who has faked ghosts for a living, talks straight to you, and has one story he could never explain away. He also gets on the tram himself. It is nearly twice the length and, I think, a good deal more frightening.

**11 November 2026. One to frighten.** The first two stories are delicate things, and I wanted one that simply sets out to scare. That is Alfred Staniforth’s trade, so “The Last Car but One” is up: a late tram in Sheffield, a passenger who was not there when it started, and the inquest afterwards. The flood at the back of it is real, and the details of that night come from a history written in 1864, the year it happened. Staniforth writes in short paragraphs with a sting in the tail, which is exactly the habit I had struck out of the other two. It turns out the popular magazines really did write like that, so for him it stays.

**11 November 2026. “The Reader”, second run.** I had the first story rewritten to take out the habits that made it sound like now: the one-line paragraphs, the lists of three, the details that all paid off. The plot is the same. It has gained a paragraph about the town, a cook with opinions and a pious sentence near the end, and it has lost its joke of a last line, which I find I don’t miss. I asked for one thing back: the opening sentence about the vicarage.

**11 November 2026. A second voice.** “The Diary at Direction Island”, by John Trembath, is up: a cable operator on a coral atoll, and a station log that goes on being kept in the wrong handwriting. The island is real and so are its rats, which come from a book of 1910 by the station’s own doctor. I told Claude that the first story still had some modern habits in it (the neat one-line paragraph, the list of three, every detail paying off), and this one was written with those struck out. Put the two side by side and they do sound like different people, which was the whole question.

**11 November 2026. The first story.** “The Reader”, by Harriet Tugwell, is up. It is set as *Chambers’s Journal* would have set it in 1900, down to the missing full stops after “Mrs”. One story proves nothing about whether ten voices can be told apart. That needs a second, by somebody as unlike her as possible.

**11 November 2026. Live, and a question about honesty.** *Milestone.* The site is public. Before the first story was written I asked an awkward question: were these ten styles really the styles of the time, or only what we now imagine the time sounded like? The answer was the second, more than I liked. So we went and read the real thing: a dozen issues of *Chambers’s Journal* from the winter of 1899, and some of *Temple Bar* from 1890. It turns out the sentences were shorter than I expected and the semicolons far thicker on the ground, that *Chambers’s* never put a full stop after “Mr”, and that it hardly printed ghost stories at all, which made George Ormiston more interesting. The stories are now written to those habits and not to mine.

**11 November 2026. A page each, and an editor.** The ten now have a biography apiece, written as a careful researcher might write them, with the invented parts and the true parts woven together. I asked for more nuance in what each of them thinks a ghost is, because a one-line slogan made them sound more certain than people are. So each page now has the slogan and then the complication: the soldier whose tidy theory is really about having survived, the lawyer who is less afraid of the dead than of being accurately described. I also wanted one thread joining them, without making them a club. That is George Ormiston, assistant editor of *Chambers’s Journal*, who asked each of them for a story at some point in 33 years and is otherwise unrecorded. He is unrecorded because I made him up, but an assistant editor on a family magazine is exactly the kind of person the record does lose.

**11 November 2026. Ten people who never lived.** *Milestone.* Started with research: the magazine market, what an unknown writer was paid (a guinea or so per thousand words, if lucky), and what people at the time thought ghosts were, which turns out to be at least eight different things. Then ten writers: five women and five men, among them a reporter, a soldier from the ranks, a classics lecturer and a cotton spinner. Each is pinned to a real place and a real event. The best accident so far: the trooper hanged at Reading in 1896, the man Wilde wrote his ballad about, turns out to have come from the Berkshire valley already chosen for the soldier. Nobody planned that.
