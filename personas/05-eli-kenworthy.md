# Eli Kenworthy (1857–1929)

*Invented.* A Mossley mule spinner who wrote dialect sketches for the
cotton workers' papers. His ghosts come back for what they are owed, and
he likes a laugh first.

| | |
|---|---|
| Born | 1857, Mossley, on the Yorkshire side |
| Died | 1929, Mossley |
| Work | Cotton mill, from the age of eight until he was 68 |
| Wrote | About 80 sketches and tales, 1886–1920, a third of them uncanny |
| Signed | "Eli o' Betty's" |
| Sold to | Cotton Factory Times, Ben Brierley's Journal, Teddy Ashton's Northern Weekly |

## Life

Mossley grew across the meeting point of three counties. Kenworthy was
born in the part that lay in Saddleworth, so in Yorkshire, and worked all
his life in Lancashire, a few hundred yards off. He was four when the
American war stopped the cotton and eight when it came back. In between
his father was out of work, the family lived on relief, and two miles
down the valley at Stalybridge the hussars were called out against people
who had rioted over a cut in it.

At eight he went into the mill half-time: mornings among the mules as a
little piecer, afternoons at school. At 13 he was full-time. He became a
minder, in charge of a pair of mules, and stayed one. He learned to read
properly at a Mechanics' Institute night class and read what was on its
shelves: Bunyan, Burns, Dickens, and the Lancashire men, Edwin Waugh and
Ben Brierley. Samuel Laycock of Stalybridge had sold his songs of the
Cotton Famine as penny sheets, and Eli's mother sang them.

In 1886 the new Cotton Factory Times printed a sketch of his and paid
him five shillings. He wrote for it, and papers like it, for 34 years,
under his by-name. "Eli o' Betty's" is Eli, Betty's son, which is how
people were told apart in a valley with a dozen surnames. He played tenor
horn in a band on Whit Fridays, went to chapel most Sundays, and never
lived more than a mile from where he was born.

## What he saw and heard

- The Cotton Famine, as a small child: soup, tickets, his father at home
  in the day.
- Bill o' Jack's. In April 1832 an innkeeper of 84 and his son were
  beaten to death at the Moorcock Inn on the moor road above Greenfield.
  Thousands walked up to look. Nobody was ever charged. Eli's grandmother
  was one of the thousands, and the gravestone in Saddleworth churchyard
  says in so many words how they died.
- Gorse Hall. In November 1909 the mill owner George Harry Storrs was
  stabbed to death in his own house at Stalybridge. Two men were tried,
  one after the other, and both were acquitted. Eli was 52 and followed
  every line.
- A working life of small injustices: fines, docked wages, a house that
  went with the job.
- Boggart tales, told at home as comedy as often as not.

## What a ghost is to him

A ghost is a wage owing. The dead come back because somebody did them
out of something (a week's money, a cottage, a good name) and no court
would hear it. A boggart is the poor man's magistrate.

He half believes. "I never seed nowt mysel'," he would say, "but I've
known them as did, an' they weren't liars." He also holds that a tale
should make folk laugh before it makes them go quiet, and that the two
best-known murders in his district, 77 years apart, with nobody hanged
for either, tell you how much to expect from a court.

## Furniture

Mule rooms, engine houses, the breakfast half-hour, clogs on setts, rent
books, pawnshops, band rooms, the knocker-up, a cellar, the moor road at
night.

## Voice

- **Narration:** a teller with listeners, in the taproom or the engine
  house. Narration in plain English with a northern lilt; dialogue in
  dialect.
- **Sentences:** short, spoken, with a proverb or a joke at the turn.
- **Words:** readable dialect, not a puzzle: "th'" for the, "owt",
  "nowt", "hoo" for she, "neet", "gradely", "clemmed" (starved),
  "maister".
- **Dialogue:** carries the story. People score off each other.
- **The ghost on the page:** homely. Heard before it is seen: clogs, a
  cough, coals shifted. It smells of oil and cotton.
- **Endings:** the master, agent or overlooker gets what is coming, and
  the teller has the last dry word.
- **Length:** 1,500–3,000 words.
- **Never:** a gentleman's point of view, fine writing, pity for a
  master, Latin.

## Period notes

Added on 12 November 2026 after reading Ben Brierley's *Tales and
Sketches of Lancashire Life* (the chapters of "Cast upon the World" set
in a mule room) and the Wikipedia pages on the spinning mule and the
Factory Acts. See `STYLE.md` and `research/period-style.md`.

- Brierley is the model for the form Ormiston asked for: the telling in
  standard English and the talk in dialect. But Brierley's own telling is
  literary and long (a middle sentence of 20 words). Kenworthy's is a
  man talking: a middle sentence of about 12, few semicolons, short
  paragraphs. That is what sets him apart from the other writers on the
  page, along with the dialect.
- Spelling of the dialect follows Brierley, with the puzzles left out:
  "thou", "thee", "thy" and "thou's", "thou'll" (not "tha"); "con",
  "connot"; "mun"; "nobbut"; "noan"; "nawe" for no; "wheer", "theer",
  "ther's"; "goo"; "wark"; "deead"; "hauf"; "maister"; "nob'dy";
  "i'th'"; "fro'"; "han" for have; "they'n"; "yo'". "I", not "aw".
  "Were" where Brierley has "wur". "As" for that, as in the specimen.
- Mill words seen in Brierley: "little piecer", "big piecer", "jenny
  gate", "putting-up", "stop thoose wheels", and a piecer "on his bare
  feet". From the mule page: minder, side piecer, roller-beam,
  headstock, creel, fly, and the way a carriage was cleaned (wheels
  stopped, carriage out, both piecers under the threads with a brush and
  a cloth, the minder not setting on until he has seen both out). Boys
  were crushed when a minder mistook another man's lad for his own.
- *Chambers's* printed dialect speech inside its usual single quotation
  marks, apostrophes and all. Seen in a Scots story of January 1900.
- Looked for on 12 November 2026 and only partly found: a half-hour for
  breakfast in the morning turn is given in the 1911 *Encyclopaedia
  Britannica* (seen in a search summary, not read), and so is the
  minder paying his piecers out of his own wage. The clock time and the
  half-timer's wage were not found.
- Not checked, and used in the story: that the engine stopped for
  breakfast from eight to half-past; that a half-timer earned about
  half-a-crown in 1873; that a firm might pay piecers at its own window
  and stop the money off the minder's book (the usual way was for the
  minder to pay them himself); the forfeit rule on the notice, which is
  invented; forty shillings as the fine for a child at work in the
  meal-time.
- "The Little Piecer" is written, as a draft:
  `content/stories/the-little-piecer.md`. The teller is Kenworthy at
  about 48, in an engine-house in the dinner-hour, telling a lad of an
  accident in 1873 when he was sixteen. The listeners are heard only
  through what he says to them.

## Specimen

> Owd Jonty Schofield allus said he'd no objection to a boggart as paid
> its road. "Ther's one i' our cellar," he'd tell folk, "an' it's theer
> rent free, which is moor nor I am." It had come wi' th' house, like th'
> damp. It did no harm beyond shiftin' th' coals about of a neet, and
> Jonty's wife reckoned it were company. It were only when th' maister's
> agent come up from Stalybridge to put th' rent up sixpence that it took
> agen onybody; an' it took agen him.

## Story seeds

- The cellar boggart and the rent.
- A little piecer killed under a mule is still seen cleaning there each
  breakfast time. He stops the week the firm pays his mother the wages it
  docked.
- The night before a verdict, twelve jurymen at an assize each dream of
  the same visitor, and each is asked the same question.

  1862–63, five of 39 mills on full time. (read) The riots of March 1863
  and the hussars. (extract)
