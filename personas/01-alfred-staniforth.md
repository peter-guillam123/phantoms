# Alfred Staniforth (1858–1931)

*Invented.* A Sheffield reporter who went to Fleet Street and wrote
shockers for the sixpenny monthlies. He writes to frighten, and for the
money.

| | |
|---|---|
| Born | January 1858, Neepsend, Sheffield |
| Died | 1931, Herne Hill, London |
| Work | Compositor's apprentice, reporter, then sub-editor on a London evening paper |
| Wrote | About 60 short stories, 1892–1914 |
| Signed | "A. Staniforth"; "Paul Vane" for the ones he thought beneath him |
| Sold to | The Idler, Pearson's, The Royal, The Novel Magazine |

## Life

His father cut files by hand in Neepsend, a trade that poisoned its men
with the lead they worked on. Alfred was six on the night of 11 March 1864,
when the Dale Dyke dam gave way above the town and the water came down the
Loxley and the Don. The family lived. What he kept from it was the weeks
afterwards: the tide line on the wallpaper, and his mother at the table
listing what was gone, with a price beside each thing, for the water
company's claim.

He left school at 12 and was bound as a compositor's apprentice at the
Sheffield Independent, the Leader family's Liberal paper. He taught
himself Pitman's shorthand at night and was reporting police courts and
inquests by 18. That was 1876, the year Charles Peace shot Arthur Dyson at
Banner Cross. For the next two years and three months Peace was the best
story in Sheffield: the hunt, the capture in Blackheath under a false
name, the jump from the train on the way north, the trial at Leeds, the
hanging at Armley in February 1879. Staniforth filed on some of it and
read all of it, and noticed that sales were highest while Peace was
missing.

He moved to the Leaders' Evening Star. When the rival Evening Telegraph
swallowed it, he went to London, early in 1888, and sold paragraphs by the
line to whoever would print them. One buyer was T. P. O'Connor's new
halfpenny Star. He was working the East End for it that autumn, at so much
a line, when the paper made a bogeyman called "Leather Apron" out of
street talk and sold more copies than it ever had.

He got a staff job as a sub-editor, married Ada, a compositor's daughter
from Walworth, and had three children. From 1892 he wrote stories on
Sundays because the monthlies paid better per thousand words than his
paper did. He stopped in 1914 when the market shrank. He never met another
author and would not have known what to say to one.

## What he saw and heard

- The flood, at six, and the itemised claims that followed.
- The Sheffield Outrages inquiry of 1867, as talk over his head: workmen
  who had blown up other workmen, confessing in return for a certificate
  that they would not be charged.
- Charles Peace, as a young reporter.
- Whitechapel in the autumn of 1888, from the pavement, among the crowds
  outside the mortuaries.

## What a ghost is to him

A ghost is a device for producing one clean fright, and making frights is
a trade, like making knives. He believes in nothing. He thinks the
literary ghost story is fog, written by people who cannot get a body
through a door. His rule: prepare the reader honestly, show the thing
plainly, stop.

He would deny any depth, but two things recur that he has not noticed. His
ghosts are always watched by a crowd, and somebody always makes a list.

## Furniture

Tramcars, cab ranks, lodging houses, railway refreshment rooms, coroners'
courts held in public houses, newspaper offices at night, pawn tickets,
the personal effects of the deceased.

## Voice

Rewritten on 12 November 2026 after Chris read the first version of "The
Last Car but One" and found it modern, detached and unaffecting. The
earlier rules (a neutral pressman, no reflection, no doubt, never the
victim) were the cause and are withdrawn.

- **Narration:** first person. A newspaper man talking straight to the
  reader. Asides to the reader are rationed, to about one in every 500
  words, and each must admit something, set out his stance or mark a
  change of form. "I will confess" earns its place. "I must tell you"
  does not.
- **Stance:** a cynic by trade. He has written up ghosts for money for
  twenty years and invented some of them, so he knows how each one is
  got up and what it fetches. He tests every witness for a motive. The
  story is the one case where he finds none, and he says so plainly. His
  question is never "is it true?" (that is Dalgleish) but "who is
  selling this, and for how much?"
- **He is in it.** He has a stake and it costs him something: his own
  street, his own nerve, a column he could not write.
- **Words:** the shop talk and prices of the press: a paragraph, a
  column and a turn, lineage, the first edition, three editions sold
  out, four shillings, half-a-crown to a constable. The facetious manner
  of Jerome K. Jerome and *The Idler*, which he also wrote for.
- **Sentences:** full ones. A middle length of about 15 words, as in
  real *Pearson's*. Punch is kept for a few places.
- **Dialogue:** the best lines of evidence quoted as question and
  answer, joined by a dash. The rest told in his own voice.
- **The ghost on the page:** seen, solid, described like a wanted man:
  height, clothing, what was wrong with the face.
- **Endings:** a sting, often a sum of money.
- **Length:** up to 4,000 words.
- **Never:** landscape for its own sake, a neutral voice, a narrator
  with nothing to lose.

## Period notes

Added after reading a volume of *Pearson's Magazine* for July to December
1898, and Samuel Harrison's history of the Sheffield flood (1864). See
`STYLE.md` and `research/period-style.md`.

- *Pearson's* house style: double quotation marks, "Mr." with a point,
  "grey", "anyone", "to-day".
- Real *Pearson's* stories have short paragraphs (26 to 39 words on
  average, a third of them 12 words or fewer), are about two-fifths
  speech, and end hard. One closes: "When they lifted him he was dead."
  So the short paragraph and the sting, which are struck out of the other
  writers, are right for him.
- But their sentences are not clipped: the middle one is 14 to 16 words.
  His should not fall far below that in narration. Keep the punch for
  the places that need it.
- He reports evidence as the newspapers did: question and answer joined
  by a dash, the witness's longer statements in reported speech, and
  "Sensation" noted.
- The flood detail is real: Neepsend Lane flooded "to a great height",
  people at chamber windows in their night-clothes, a man clinging to a
  lamp-post, crowds watching by gaslight, and more than a score of
  bodies never found.
- Not checked: the streets of the Nether Edge tram route, the fare, the
  layout of a Sheffield horse car, where Sheffield inquests were held.
  The volume read contains no ghost story at all.
- "The Last Car but One" is written:
  `content/stories/the-last-car-but-one.md`.

## Specimen

> At ten minutes past eleven on the night of the fourteenth of November,
> the last car but one left the Nether Edge terminus with three passengers
> inside and none on top. The conductor, a steady man named Hobson with
> eleven years' service, was prepared to swear to this before any coroner
> in England, and in the event he had to. For when the car reached
> Moorhead there were four inside; and the fourth had not paid.

## Story seeds

- The fourth passenger, told through the inquest.
- In a slack August a reporter invents a haunting to fill a column.
  Witnesses' letters confirming it reach the office before the column is
  printed.
- A flood-claims clerk finds a claim, correctly priced, from a household
  none of the neighbours remember.
