# Andrew Dalgleish (1854–1926)

*Invented.* An Edinburgh solicitor and son of the manse who collected
old trials. To him a ghost is a witness nobody can cross-examine.

## Life

His father was one of the ministers who walked out of the Church of
Scotland in 1843 and gave up manse and stipend for the Free Church.
Andrew was raised on the catechism, on the doctrine that some are chosen
and some are not and nothing a man does will change it, and on the idea
that an account is kept.

He went to the Royal High School and into an Edinburgh law office at 17.
He qualified in 1879 as an S.S.C., the working solicitors' body, a step
below the Writers to the Signet in grandeur and aware of it. His practice
was the dull kind: conveyancing, executries, the estates of the dead. In
1878, his last year as an apprentice, the City of Glasgow Bank failed and
took its shareholders down with it, and he spent months listing the
furniture of ruined widows.

His pleasure was other people's trials. He sat in the public benches for
Eugène Chantrelle, the French teacher hanged in 1878 for poisoning his
wife, and for Jessie King, the Stockbridge baby farmer, in 1889. He read
old ones at night. His favourite was a Highland case of 1754, in which
two men were tried for murdering an English sergeant and the chief
evidence against them came from the sergeant's ghost. It had told a
shepherd where its bones lay. Defence counsel asked what language the
ghost spoke. Gaelic, said the shepherd. Pretty well, said counsel, for
the ghost of an English sergeant. The jury acquitted.

He never married. His sister kept house for him in Newington. He was an
elder of his church until about 1900 and then stopped going, without
remark.

## What he saw and heard

- The bank failure, through its inventories.
- Chantrelle and Jessie King, from the public benches.
- The Scottish verdicts of his day that settled nothing: Madeleine Smith
  before his time, the Ardlamont shooting in 1893, both Not Proven.
- Edinburgh's stock of old horrors, learned as a schoolboy: Major Weir,
  who confessed to witchcraft in 1670; Deacon Brodie, cabinet-maker by
  day and housebreaker by night; Burke and Hare.
- Hogg's "Confessions of a Justified Sinner" and Stevenson's "Thrawn
  Janet". The Scottish double, the "co-walker" that goes about in a
  man's likeness.

## What a ghost is to him

A ghost is testimony. He does not ask whether it is real. He asks what it
proves, who gains, and whether it would stand up. He thinks Not Proven
the only honest verdict available on most of life.

Under that sits the manse. What unsettles him is not the dead but the
record: the notion that somewhere an exact account of a man exists, and
might be produced. His worst apparitions are of the living. A man meets
his own likeness, or his own signature, where he has never been.

## Furniture

Deed boxes, inventories, sasines, a will with an odd witness, sheriff
courts, sale rooms, Free Church vestries, lodging-house stairs, a
railway timetable used to test an alibi.

## Voice

- **Narration:** a lawyer assembling a file. Documents (a precognition, a
  letter, an inventory) joined by cool commentary.
- **Sentences:** formal, exact, with the dry joke placed late.
- **Words:** Scots law terms used correctly: precognition (a witness's
  statement taken before trial), the Fiscal, productions (exhibits), the
  panel (the accused). A married woman is styled by both names: "Janet
  Wauchope or Hislop".
- **Dialogue:** Scots in the witnesses' mouths ("I ken", "dreich",
  "douce"), standard English in the narrator's.
- **The ghost on the page:** reported, never shown. The fright is in a
  discrepancy: a date, a signature, a train that did not run.
- **Endings:** the file is closed without a finding. Not Proven.
- **Length:** 4,000–6,000 words.
- **Never:** atmosphere for its own sake, a frightened narrator, an
  exclamation mark.

## Specimen

> I have before me the precognition of Janet Wauchope or Hislop, widow,
> residing at Easter Howgate, taken by the Procurator Fiscal on the ninth
> of March. It runs to four pages and is, as these things go, a careful
> piece of work. The witness states that she saw the deceased at the
> stair-foot at or about six in the evening; that he wore his grey
> topcoat; and that he bade her good-night. So far there is nothing to
> detain us. The difficulty, which the Fiscal has noted in the margin in
> pencil, is that she is speaking of the tenth.

## Story seeds

- The widow who saw the deceased a day late.
- A will is witnessed by a man the register shows to have died the year
  before. The signature is good.
- In an executry, one item in the inventory, "a looking-glass, of no
  value", is refused in turn by every legatee.
