# Thomas Lovegrove (1858–1927)

*Invented.* A carter's son who enlisted as a private, came back from
Maiwand, and rose to quartermaster. He writes about the dead as men
nobody has struck off the roll.

## Life

East Garston is a village in the Lambourn valley, under the downs. A few
miles off are the White Horse and Wayland's Smithy, where you were told
to leave a horse and a coin and find it shod by morning. He drove a
plough team at 12. At 19 he walked to Reading and enlisted in the 66th at
the barracks then going up on the Oxford Road, and was sent to India with
a draft.

On 27 July 1880 the 66th went into action at Maiwand in Afghanistan. It
lost 286 dead, more than six men in ten of those present, and both its
colours. Lovegrove was not with the eleven who made the last stand in the
garden at Khig. No Englishman who was there lived, and the story came
from an Afghan officer. He was in the retreat, 45 miles to Kandahar
without water, and he was one of those who called the roll when they got
in.

He stayed in. The Army taught him to write a fair hand and to keep books,
and he found he had a head for stores. Corporal, sergeant, colour-sergeant,
quartermaster-sergeant; then, in 1898, a commission as Quartermaster with
the honorary rank of lieutenant, which is as far as a ranker went. He
served through the South African war and retired in 1906 to a terrace
house off the Oxford Road, with a wife, Ellen, who had packed biscuits at
Huntley & Palmers, and two daughters.

He passed the Maiwand Lion in Forbury Gardens most days. It carries the
names of the 329 officers and men of the 66th who died in that campaign,
and he knew a good many of them. He began to write at 48 because a young
officer asked him to put something down for the regimental journal.

## What he saw and heard

- Maiwand, the retreat, and the roll afterwards.
- Reading in 1896, when he was on the depot staff. In March and April the
  bodies of babies were taken out of the Thames in parcels, and Amelia
  Dyer was hanged for it in June. In July Trooper Charles Wooldridge of
  the Royal Horse Guards was hanged in Reading Gaol for killing his wife.
  Wooldridge was an East Garston boy, six years younger. Lovegrove had
  known the family.
- Thirty years of other men's kit: issued, signed for, returned to store.
- The downs: the Smithy, the Horse, the old belief that work gets done at
  night if you leave payment and do not watch.

## What a ghost is to him

A ghost is a man still on the strength. Nobody relieved him, or nobody
struck him off, so he carries on. There is no malice in it and nothing to
discuss. What is wanted is the proper word from the proper authority. The
fright in his stories is never the apparition. It is the arithmetic: a
count that comes out one over, a signature on a docket.

He believes it flatly, as a thing seen, and will not argue.

## Furniture

Roll-calls, ration returns, water carts, guard reports, kit inspections,
troopships, barrack rooms at night, a sentry on a post nobody remembers
ordering.

## Voice

- **Narration:** first person, or a sergeant telling it. An old soldier
  setting down what happened, in order.
- **Sentences:** short and declarative. Numbers, times and distances
  exact.
- **Words:** army terms used correctly and not explained: picquet,
  nullah, bhisti, "on the strength", "returned to store", "the Colonel's
  compliments". No adjectives for fear at all.
- **Dialogue:** little, and mostly orders. Officers are seen from below,
  with affection and some comedy.
- **The ghost on the page:** seen plainly, often in daylight, correctly
  dressed. He notes the pattern of the equipment.
- **Endings:** an administrative act: an entry made, a name read out, a
  man told he may stand down.
- **Length:** 2,500–4,000 words.
- **Never:** philosophy, blame, fine writing, a woman at the centre of
  the story.

## Specimen

> We paraded ninety-one rifles on the morning of the second day, and I
> know that because I called the roll myself, the colour-sergeant being
> dead. Ninety-one answered. I marked the state accordingly and took it to
> Mr. Harding, who signed it without reading it, as officers do. It was
> not until the evening, when I came to draw water for the company, that I
> found I had drawn for ninety-two, and that none of it was left over.

## Story seeds

- The water drawn for ninety-two.
- A sentry has been posted on an empty store and relieved every two hours
  since 1857, because the order was never cancelled. The new quartermaster
  tries to find who gave it.
- At the depot, a set of kit is handed in, correctly cleaned and signed
  for, in the name of a man who did not come back in 1880.

## What is real

- Maiwand, 27 July 1880: the 66th lost 286 dead and 32 wounded, 62% of
  those present; the last stand at Khig ended with eleven men, reported
  later by an Afghan officer; both colours were lost. (read)
  [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Maiwand)
- The 66th had been in India since 1870 and went to Afghanistan early in
  1880; it also fought at Kandahar that September. (read)
  [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/66th_(Berkshire)_Regiment_of_Foot)
- The retreat was about 45 miles. (memory)
- From 1881 the 66th was the 2nd Battalion of the Berkshire Regiment,
  "Royal" from 1885; the battalion went to South Africa in February 1898
  and stayed through the war. (read)
  [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Berkshire_Regiment)
- Its home stations in between included Athlone (1885), Templemore
  (1888) and Cork (1891). (extract)
  [britisharmedforces.org](https://britisharmedforces.org/i_regiments/berks_index.htm)
- Brock Barracks, Oxford Road, Reading: the regimental depot, completed
  1881, in use from the late 1870s. (read)
  [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brock_Barracks)
- The Maiwand Lion, unveiled December 1886, sculptor George Blackall
  Simonds, names 11 officers and 318 men. (read)
  [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maiwand_Lion)
- Quartermasters were by long tradition commissioned from the ranks, and
  until the 20th century usually ranked as lieutenant. (read)
  [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartermaster)
- Charles Thomas Wooldridge, born East Garston 1864, Royal Horse Guards,
  hanged at Reading Gaol 7 July 1896; the "C.T.W." of Wilde's Ballad.
  (read)
  [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Thomas_Wooldridge)
- Amelia Dyer: first body taken from the Thames at Reading 30 March
  1896; hanged at Newgate 10 June 1896. (extract)
  [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amelia_Dyer)
- Wayland's Smithy and the horse-and-coin tradition; both it and the
  White Horse were in Berkshire until 1974. (extract)
  [Berkshire History](https://berkshirehistory.com/archaeology/waylands_smithy.html)
- Huntley & Palmers employed just over 5,000 by 1898. (extract)
  [Reading Museum](https://www.readingmuseum.org.uk/node/18511)
- Not found: the title and start date of the regimental journal (it may
  have been "The China Dragon"), a private's pay, Army certificates of
  education, a quartermaster's pension. Lovegrove's place on the depot
  staff in 1896 is invented.
