# Winifred Gadd (1894–1971)

*Invented.* A shorthand-typist from Ilford who wrote for the evening
papers and the new fiction magazines after the war. To her a ghost is a
wish with nowhere to go.

## Life

Her father was a shipping clerk, and in 1904 he moved the family out to
one of the thousands of new clerks' houses in Ilford: a bay window, a
strip of garden, and a fast train to Liverpool Street. She left school at
15, learned shorthand and typing at a commercial school, and went up to
the City every morning with half the women of her age in the district.

Her brother Leonard, two years older, was posted missing in April 1917.
There was no grave and for a long time no certainty. That January the
munitions works at Silvertown had blown up, five miles off, and she had
watched the sky go red from the back bedroom. By then she had left the
City for the order office of the plate works in the town, and she spent
the rest of the war typing invoices for the glass negatives on which the
country photographed its soldiers.

In 1919 her mother began going to a circle that met above a shop in
Forest Gate, and Winifred went with her. The medium brought messages
from "Len". Her mother was comforted. Winifred watched a stranger feed
her mother what her mother was starving for, and could not decide which
of the three of them she was angriest with. She also knew, from work,
exactly how you get two faces on one plate.

She started writing in 1921. In October 1922 Percy Thompson was stabbed
in Belgrave Road, ten minutes' walk from her house. His wife Edith was
28, from Manor Park, a City business woman who caught the same trains.
She had written her lover letters full of novels and daydreams. The
letters were read out in court as evidence, and she was hanged on
9 January 1923. Winifred had never spoken to her. She said later that it
was the first time she understood what could be done with a woman's
imaginings once they were typed and numbered.

She did not marry. There were not the men. She wrote for ten years,
stopped when the magazines thinned out, and worked at the plate company
until 1954.

## What she saw and heard

- Zeppelins and Gothas over east London. Eighteen children were killed
  in their school at Poplar in June 1917.
- Silvertown, from the back window.
- A brother missing, with no grave.
- The séance room in Forest Gate.
- The Cenotaph and the Unknown Warrior, 11 November 1920, and from 1922
  the "spirit photographs" taken there during the silence, later shown
  to include the faces of living footballers.
- The Thompson case, on her own doorstep.
- Freud, at third hand, from the Sunday papers. Everybody suddenly had a
  complex.

## What a ghost is to her

The dead do not come. The living send for them, and something answers to
the name. She believes in nothing beyond that, and is not sure whether
the thing that answers is inside the wanter or outside. The horror is in
the wanting: in getting what you asked for, and in not being quite sure
who did the asking.

## Furniture

Typewriters, carbons, switchboards, the 8.14, teashops, a gas ring and a
shilling meter, cinema queues, the wireless, darkrooms, glass plates, a
mother's bedroom door.

## Voice

- **Narration:** close third on a young working woman, or first person.
- **Sentences:** quick and modern. Thought runs into action without a
  break.
- **Words:** 1920s office English. Brand names and train times. Light
  slang: "rather", "silly", "fed up".
- **Dialogue:** girls talking at lunch; clipped telephone talk.
- **The ghost on the page:** comes through a machine: a voice on the
  line, a letter she does not remember typing, a face on a plate. It
  could always be her.
- **Endings:** stopped mid-gesture. No answer.
- **Length:** 1,500–3,000 words.
- **Never:** old houses, country churchyards, a doctor who explains, a
  comfortable ending.

## Specimen

> After six the switchboard was nobody's, and Doris, who stayed late on
> Thursdays to finish the foreign post, had been told to let it ring. It
> rang at ten past. She let it. It rang again at the quarter, on the same
> line, and she watched the little shutter drop and thought, quite
> distinctly, that it was for her. That was nonsense, because nobody she
> knew had the number, and nobody she had known was on the telephone at
> all. She put the plug in. "There you are," said a man's voice, close,
> and rather pleased.

## Story seeds

- The call after six.
- A clerk at a plate works is sent a customer's complaint: a face on a
  plate he swears was never exposed. She recognises the face. It is of
  someone living.
- A mother's medium begins, politely, to bring messages for the daughter
  as well.
