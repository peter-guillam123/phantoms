# The ten writers

All ten are invented. The places, events, magazines and beliefs around
them are real, and each file ends with a "What is real" list that says
how well each fact has been checked.

## What a ghost is, to each of them

Each has a one-line idea and a complication under it. The line is what
they would say. The complication is what the stories show. A story should
serve both. The fullest version of each is on the site, in
`content/writers/`, under "What a ghost was to him" or "to her".

1. **Staniforth: a device for one clean fright.** He says he believes
   nothing and writes for money. But his ghosts always appear before a
   crowd and somebody always makes a list: the flood claims of his
   childhood. Fear, for him, is public and has a price.
2. **Tugwell: what a household will not say.** The dead are with God, so
   what walks is shared, unspoken knowledge. She is not simply for
   speaking out. Silence also holds a house up, and her outsiders leave
   without exposing anything.
3. **Lovegrove: a man still on the strength.** No malice, only a soldier
   nobody stood down. Underneath is survivor's arithmetic: he was counted
   in at Kandahar, and his stories keep putting the numbers right.
4. **Charlton: an ancient debt, unpaid.** Not moral: the dead want what is
   due, in the proper form. She half envies the ancients, who knew the
   form. A woman denied her degree knows about dues withheld, though she
   would wave that away.
5. **Kenworthy: a wage owing.** The boggart as the poor man's magistrate.
   But the claims are small (sixpence, a week's pay), the ghost does what
   a chapel-going spinner would not dare, and it is funny, and company.
6. **Tobin: a neighbour in need of a favour.** The dead are harmless and
   are owed prayers. The fear is the living, once they are sure. After
   1895 she can neither mock the old beliefs nor romanticise them.
7. **Dalgleish: a witness nobody can cross-examine.** He asks what it
   proves, not whether it is true. Underneath is the manse: fear of an
   exact record of himself. His worst apparitions are doubles of the
   living. He leaves cases open because a verdict would be on someone.
8. **Protheroe: a warning that comes first.** The omen comes before the
   death. The old story calls it a mercy, so people can prepare. In hers
   nobody prepares: there is only knowing, and watching, as a district
   once watched Sarah Jacob.
9. **Gadd: a wish with nowhere to go.** The living send for the dead and
   something answers. She cannot settle whether it is inside the wanter
   or outside. Her second fear is her own mind produced as evidence, as
   Edith Thompson's letters were.
10. **Trembath: a message that outlived its sender.** Not souls, only
    traffic that persists. The tapping is neutral, and the terrible part
    is that a trained man cannot help reading it. He finds this steadying.

Five women, five men. One journalist, one soldier, one academic. Two with
no schooling past 13 (Kenworthy, Lovegrove), and three more who learned
their trade on the job (Staniforth, Gadd, Trembath).

## Pairs that could blur, and what keeps them apart

- **Staniforth and Dalgleish** both use inquests and evidence. Staniforth
  wants speed and a body on the floor. Dalgleish wants a discrepancy on
  paper and no body at all.
- **Tugwell and Gadd** both locate the haunting in a mind or a family.
  Tugwell's people will not speak. Gadd's people cannot stop thinking.
  Tugwell's sentences are long and settled; Gadd's are quick.
- **Charlton and Tobin** both write about what the living owe the dead.
  For Charlton it is a contract with something old and indifferent. For
  Tobin it is a kindness between neighbours, and the dead are the least
  of anyone's worries.
- **Lovegrove and Trembath** are both plain men writing reports.
  Lovegrove's dead are comrades and want a word of release. Trembath's
  are not persons at all, only traffic.
- **Kenworthy and Tobin** are both funny and both write talk well.
  Kenworthy's ghost takes a side in a quarrel about money. Tobin's ghost
  is harmless and the danger is the crowd.
- **Protheroe and Lovegrove** both state things flatly. Protheroe's
  events lie ahead and cannot be stopped. Lovegrove's lie behind and can
  be put right.

## Where the dread could sit

Chris, 13 November 2026, after four stories: "I do want to make sure
some of our corpus has a real sense of dread or malevolence or threat."

What follows is a proposal put to him that day. It is not agreed.

The first four are a delicate one, a steady one, a fright that ends in
pity and a funny one. None has a ghost that means harm. That comes from
how the ten were built: each ghost has a reason (a wage, a debt, a
message), and a ghost with a reason is hard to be afraid of. Of the six
still to write:

| Writer | What it could carry | How |
|---|---|---|
| Charlton | Malevolence | Something old that is owed and collects. It is not angry, which is worse. Somebody should be hurt, in the body. |
| Gadd | Threat | The thing that answers is not the one who was sent for, and it wants in. |
| Tobin | Menace from the living | No harm from the dead at all. A kitchen full of neighbours who are sure. |
| Protheroe | Dread | The death is known a week ahead and cannot be stopped. |
| Dalgleish | Cold dread | A double of a living man, on paper, where he has never been. |
| Lovegrove | None | His dead are comrades. He is the relief. |

So three of the six would be written to frighten in earnest, each in a
different way, and two more to leave a chill.

## Places where their lives could cross

None of this is needed. It is here in case the project grows a story of
its own.

- Lovegrove's battalion was stationed at Templemore, Co. Tipperary, in
  1888, 30 miles from Nora Tobin's shop.
- Amelia Dyer was tried at the Old Bailey in May 1896. Staniforth could
  have been on the press bench while Lovegrove was in Reading, where the
  bodies were found.
- Dalgleish and Charlton both appeared unsigned in Blackwood's. Neither
  could have known who the other was.
- Lovegrove and Trembath both sold to Chambers's Journal after 1906, and
  both to Blackwood's, which liked men who had served abroad.
- Tugwell's magazines all closed between 1899 and 1906, just as
  Staniforth's were starting up. One generation of ghost story replaced
  another, and they are it.
- E. F. Benson, a real and famous ghost-story writer, wrote an article
  on the Bridget Cleary case in June 1895. Nora Tobin would have read it.
- Gadd died in 1971. She is the only one of the ten a living person could
  have met.

## The editor

Chris took up the idea of a shared editor, with two changes: he works for
a real magazine, and the ten did not write only for him. He commissioned
each of them at some point.

He is [George Ormiston](00-george-ormiston.md) (1862–1934), invented,
assistant editor of the real *Chambers's Journal* in Edinburgh from 1896
to 1929. That file has his life, his taste, the story he took first from
each writer, and the real facts about the magazine.

## Story titles used on the site

The biographies name two stories for each writer: the one the specimen
paragraph comes from, and the first one Ormiston took. All twenty titles
are invented and are now promises. The full list is in

## Open questions for Chris

- **Em dashes in stories.** Period prose is full of them. I have kept
  them out of the specimens, as the house style says, but in a few
  voices (Gadd, Staniforth) their absence may show. Your call.
- **Names.** I ran one quick search for clashes with real authors and
  found none. That is a light check, not a proper one.
- **The empire.** Nobody here is Anglo-Indian or colonial, though the
  period's magazines were full of such writers. Trembath and Lovegrove
  carry some of it. Say if you want an eleventh.
