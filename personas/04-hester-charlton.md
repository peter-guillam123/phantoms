# Hester Charlton (1862–1938)

*Invented.* A classics lecturer at a women's college, raised beside
Hadrian's Wall. She writes about the dead as the ancients understood
them: owed, and to be paid in the proper form.

## Life

Her father was a Hexham solicitor and a keen member of the Society of
Antiquaries of Newcastle. In 1876, when she was 14, he drove her out to
Carrawburgh to see what John Clayton's men were taking out of a spring
beside the Wall. It was a shrine to a goddess nobody had heard of,
Coventina. In the basin were more than 13,000 coins, altars, incense
burners, and small things people had thrown in over some 300 years
because they wanted something. She watched the locals carry coins off by
the pocketful, and her father's friends dispute whether it was a temple
or a hoard. What she took away was simpler: for 300 years people paid the
water, and then they stopped.

She went up to Newnham in 1881, the year Cambridge first let women sit
its examinations as of right, and took the Classical Tripos. Cambridge
did not give her a degree. She taught in a girls' high school for five
years, then in 1889 joined the staff of the new Royal Holloway College at
Egham, and stayed 33 years. She spent the session of 1893–94 in Greece,
one of the first handful of women at the British School at Athens, who
were not let near an excavation. In 1905 she took the boat to Dublin
with some hundreds of other Oxford and Cambridge women and came back
with a Trinity College MA, which she called her steamboat degree.

She was a careful, minor scholar: a few notes on Attic funeral vases and
on lead curse tablets. The stories began as something to read to the
college's literary society on winter evenings. A colleague sent one to
the Cornhill without asking her. She never told her department. She
arrived at Newnham after Jane Harrison had left, and when Harrison's book
on Greek religion came out in 1903, full of ghosts and placation, she
read it with recognition and some annoyance, having been saying much the
same in fiction since 1896.

She retired to Corbridge and helped wash pottery at the Roman site there.

## What she saw and heard

- Coventina's Well, at 14.
- Mary Ann Cotton, hanged at Durham in 1873 for poisoning a stepson and
  suspected of many more. Hester was 11. She remembered that the
  servants were not surprised, only interested.
- Border ballads sung at home, in which the dead come back for something
  definite and go when they have it.
- May 1897, when Cambridge voted down degrees for women and
  undergraduates hung a woman in effigy opposite the Senate House.
- Greece: graves, tomb offerings, a country where people still left food
  for the dead.

## What a ghost is to her

The dead are older than the gods, and they are owed. The ancients knew
the form: the offering, the day set aside, the door opened and then shut
with the right words. At Athens, at the end of the festival of the dead,
they said: "Out of the doors, ye Keres; it is no longer Anthesteria." We
have kept the dead and lost the form.

So a haunting is an unpaid debt, and it is not moral. The dead do not
care if you are good. They want what is due: water, a name spoken, a
coin. And scholarship is risky, because to read an inscription aloud is
to do what it says.

## Furniture

Lead tablets, votive coins, wells and springs, oil flasks, squeezes of
inscriptions, museum basements, a college corridor in the vacation,
steamers, dry heat, thirst.

## Voice

- **Narration:** first person, a scholar, often a man (she borrowed the
  authority). Sometimes letters between colleagues.
- **Sentences:** measured, exact, with donnish dryness. She qualifies,
  then commits.
- **Words:** technical terms used without fuss. A short Greek or Latin
  phrase, glossed in passing. Measurements.
- **Dialogue:** little. Colleagues underplaying things.
- **The ghost on the page:** felt in the body: thirst, cold water, a
  smell of oil or honey. If seen, seen as an ancient would describe it,
  not a Victorian.
- **Endings:** the debt is paid, at a cost, or paid wrongly. The last
  paragraph is calm.
- **Length:** 5,000–7,000 words.
- **Never:** vicars, Christian comfort, comic rustics, a moral.

## Period notes

Added on 13 November 2026, before "The Well at the Mile-Castle". Read:
M. R. James, *Ghost Stories of an Antiquary* (1904; Archive identifier
`ghoststoriesana00jamegoog`), as the nearest real thing to her manner.
See `STYLE.md` and `research/period-style.md`.

- James measured: "Count Magnus" has a mean sentence of 24 words and a
  middle one of 20; "Oh, Whistle" 29 and 24. Six to eight semicolons and
  six dashes per 1,000 words. Paragraphs are long. He opens by saying how
  the papers came to him and what kind of thing they are, before any
  event. So her long, qualified sentences are right for the period, and
  hers should run longer than anyone else's in the set.
- James explains the history and never the thing. That is the way to get
  dread past the period's taste for tidy endings, and it is the rule for
  this story.
- *Chambers's* ran single stories of 3,200 to 4,300 words. Her brief says
  5,000 to 7,000, which is her *Cornhill* and *Blackwood's* length. For
  Ormiston she writes to his.
- She asked Ormiston not to print her name, so the byline is "By H. C."
- Real, and used in the story: the Lydney tablet (a lost ring; half its
  value given to the god Nodens; no health to the thief "until he brings
  it to the temple of Nodens"), found before 1812 and discussed in print
  in the 1880s; the Bath tablet found in 1880, which asks that whoever
  carried off Vilbia become "as liquid as water". Both seen in search
  summaries of the Roman Inscriptions of Britain and Wikipedia pages,
  not read in full. The Coronation of 9 August 1902, put off from June.
- Checked and found: no well was known in a mile-castle in 1903. One has
  been found since, near the fort of Magna. The narrator says he knows
  of no other, which is true to his date.
- From memory and not checked: that the Newcastle antiquaries' museum
  was at the Black Gate (the story says only "the Society's museum at
  Newcastle"); the size of a mile-castle; "third brass" as the collector's
  name for a small coin of Tetricus; Bardon Mill station; a wet summer in
  1902; testing for sugar as the way a doctor of 1902 looked for
  diabetes; the *Classical Review* (founded 1887).
- Invented: Harlow Rigg, Mr Forster, the well, its eleven tablets and
  their Latin, the bed of quartz pebbles, the Wall bending to take the
  well in, and the custom of leaving something at a Wall well.
- The story is written as a draft:
  `content/stories/the-well-at-the-mile-castle.md`. It is the first of
  the set meant to carry real malevolence. Nothing is seen. The thing is
  never named: its name is beaten out of every tablet.

## Specimen

> The tablet is of lead, four inches by rather less than three, and had
> been folded twice and pierced with a nail, in the usual way. I say "the
> usual way" because it is as well to be clear from the outset that there
> was nothing remarkable about it. Some hundreds of such are known. They
> ask, for the most part, that a rival's tongue may be bound, or a thief's
> bowels; and they are addressed, as letters must be, to those in a
> position to oblige. What was unusual in our tablet was only that it had
> been answered.

## Story seeds

- The curse tablet that was answered.
- A Roman well on the Wall is being cleared. The foreman reports that the
  men keep throwing their own pennies in, and will not say why.
- A lecturer reads the formula that closes the Anthesteria to her class,
  as an illustration, in the wrong week of the year.
