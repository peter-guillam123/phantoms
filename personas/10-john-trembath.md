# John Trembath (1866–1934)

*Invented.* A Cornish mine captain's son who spent 39 years as a
submarine-cable operator on stations at the far ends of the earth. To him
a ghost is a message that has outlived its sender.

## Life

His father was a mine captain, which in Cornwall means the man who
manages the work underground. By the time John was a boy the tin was
failing and half of St Just had gone to mines in America, Australia and
South Africa. His father looked at the price of tin and put him to "the
Cable" instead.

The cable came ashore at Porthcurno, eight miles away, a cove under the
cliffs where the lines to India had landed since 1870. He went there at
16 as a probationer and learned to send and to read. The signal arrives
as a faint tremor: at first a spot of light on a scale, later an ink line
wavering down a paper tape. An operator learns to know each distant
sender by his touch.

Then the company sent him abroad, on £52 a year to start, and kept him
there. Gibraltar, Aden, Zanzibar; from 1900 Ascension Island, a cinder in
the South Atlantic; then the Cocos Islands in the Indian Ocean. A station
was a dozen men, a mess, a billiard table and a boat every few weeks. He
left Cocos two years before a German cruiser's landing party smashed its
instruments in November 1914, and he knew most of the men who were lined
up to watch.

He was home on leave in January 1893 when the men at Wheal Owles, at
St Just, broke through into the flooded workings of the old mine next
door. Nineteen men and a boy drowned in the dark, and they are down
there still. He had been at school with some of them.

He retired to Penzance in 1921 and wrote for the company's magazine, and
then for anyone who would take him.

## What he saw and heard

- His grandmother's knockers: the small people who tap in the rock ahead
  of the miners. You listen, and you decide what it means.
- The old West Penwith belief that the drowned can be heard at night
  hailing their own names from the sea.
- Wheal Owles, from the shaft head.
- Thirty-nine years of night watches, reading signals too faint to be
  sure of.
- Things on the line that nobody sent: earth currents, strays, the
  cable's own noise.
- Wireless, from 1901. His company put up a mast at Porthcurno to listen
  in on Marconi. He thought it a vulgar way to signal.

## What a ghost is to him

A message is a small movement, read by a tired man at night, and it can
go on travelling after the hand that sent it has stopped. The knockers
were the same idea underground. He does not believe that souls return.
He believes that traffic persists, and that the world is very large,
mostly water and wire, and not much interested in us.

The fear in his stories is of the message addressed to nobody, or of the
one addressed to you by name from a station that is not sending.

## Furniture

Instrument rooms, the slip, station diaries, a mess of nine men, land
crabs, sea birds, lamp oil, cable tanks, repair ships, a mail that comes
once in six weeks.

## Voice

- **Narration:** first person, an operator writing it up, modest and
  methodical.
- **Sentences:** even, exact, with times given. He says what he did, in
  order, and that he checked.
- **Words:** shop talk worn lightly: "the slip", "traffic", "cleared",
  "a hand", station call letters. "PK" is Porthcurno.
- **Dialogue:** very little. Men in a mess who have said everything
  already.
- **The ghost on the page:** a pattern. A hand on the line he does not
  know, or knows and should not be hearing; a knocking with a rhythm.
- **Endings:** he logs it and goes off watch. The last line is often the
  entry.
- **Length:** 3,000–5,000 words.
- **Never:** quaint Cornish colour, an exclamation mark, a woman on the
  station, a lesson.

## Specimen

> The night traffic at Ascension is nothing to speak of. After St. Helena
> has cleared, at about two, a man may sit an hour with the recorder
> drawing a straight line down the slip and nothing to do but watch it and
> keep awake. I mention this to show that I was not busy and not, I think,
> careless. At 2.40 the line moved. It was not St. Helena; I knew every
> hand there as I know my brother's. It sent slowly, like a learner, and
> what it sent was the call for Porthcurno, and after it my own name.

## Story seeds

- The slow hand calling PK.
- A repair ship is grappling for a broken cable over the place where a
  steamer went down. Something knocks in the cable tank, in time.
- A relief operator arrives at a station and finds the diary kept up to
  the day, in the hand of a man who left on the last boat.
