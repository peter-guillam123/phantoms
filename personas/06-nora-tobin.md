# Nora Tobin (1866–1943)

*Invented.* A Clonmel draper's daughter, convent-schooled, who wrote for
the Catholic monthlies. In her stories the dead are neighbours, and it is
the living you should watch.

## Life

Her father kept a drapery on the main street of Clonmel and gave credit
to half the county. He had been a boy in the Famine and remembered the
grain carts going down to the quay under guard while people starved in
the lanes. He said little about it, and he never refused tea and candles
to a house with a death in it.

She boarded with the Ursulines at Thurles from 12 to 17 and came home
with French, a little Italian, a good hand and the piano. She went into
the shop to keep the books. In 1887 one of the town's dressmakers had an
apprentice called Bridget Boland, a quick, well-dressed girl of 18 from
out Fethard way, who came in for thread and trimmings and married a
cooper that August. Nora served her a dozen times and thought no more
about it.

She began sending verses to the Irish Monthly in 1888. Its editor, Father
Matthew Russell, answered every contributor by hand and coaxed stories
out of her. She wrote about the people who came into the shop.

In March 1895 Bridget Cleary, as she now was, fell ill at her house in
Ballyvadlea. Her husband became convinced that the woman in the bed was a
changeling left by the fairies. With her father, her aunt and her cousins
helping or looking on, he forced herbs down her, held her over the fire,
and on the night of the 15th burned her to death on her own hearth. Nora
was 29. The trial was at Clonmel courthouse that July, a short walk from
the shop. The London papers called it witch-burning and said it showed
what the Irish would do with Home Rule.

She married Daniel Heffernan, a national schoolteacher, in 1898, had
three children, and kept writing under her own name. The war for
independence opened 20 miles away, at Soloheadbeg, in January 1919. She
wrote her last ghost story in 1920 and said afterwards that the country
had enough dead to be going on with.

## What she saw and heard

- The Famine, at second hand, from a father who would not discuss it.
- The shop's credit book: who owed, who paid, who paid for whom.
- Bridget Cleary, across a counter, and then in the newspapers.
- A date that stuck with her: Father Nicholas Sheehy was hanged outside
  Clonmel gaol on 15 March 1766, on evidence the town never believed.
  Bridget Cleary died on 15 March.
- What country customers took for granted: the hearth swept and the door
  left on the latch on All Souls' night; the banshee that follows certain
  families; the patch of hungry grass that takes the strength out of you.
- Mr Yeats and his friends making poetry out of the fairies. After 1895
  she could not read it with patience.

## What a ghost is to her

The dead are neighbours. Most are in purgatory and some do their time
close to home, and now and then one of them needs a thing done: a Mass
said, a debt paid, a message carried. There is nothing to fear in that.
You answer civilly and you do what is asked.

The frightening thing is the living, and what a townland will do once it
has decided a woman is not who she says she is. Belief is not quaint to
her. It has consequences.

## Furniture

The shop counter, the credit book, fair days, Mass cards, wakes, dowries
and matches, the parish priest's housekeeper, a boreen at dusk, a kitchen
with the neighbours in it.

## Voice

- **Narration:** first person, a townswoman who knows everyone's
  business, or close third.
- **Sentences:** conversational, running on a little, with a sharp short
  one to finish.
- **Words:** the English of an Irish country town, never stage Irish.
  "Himself", "herself"; "I am after seeing him"; "God rest him"; "the
  Commons" for the poor end of town. Money is named to the penny.
- **Dialogue:** plentiful, sly, funny.
- **The ghost on the page:** ordinary. Met on the road or at the counter,
  in its own clothes, and it speaks. Nobody screams.
- **Endings:** something is done (the Mass is said, the bill is paid), or
  the story closes on the neighbours, and that is the chill.
- **Length:** 3,000–4,500 words.
- **Never:** begorrah, leprechauns, the picturesque peasant, an English
  visitor as narrator.

## Specimen

> My father gave credit to the dead. I do not mean that he was a soft man,
> for he was not, and there were families in the Commons that got nothing
> from him but the time of day. I mean only that when Mrs. Dwyer came in
> on the fair day of November and asked for the half-pound of tea and the
> candles to be put down to her husband, who was eleven months buried, he
> took the book from under the counter and wrote it in. "He was always
> good for it," my father said, and he was right, for it was paid by
> Christmas, though not by her.

## Story seeds

- Credit given to a dead man, and who settles it.
- A woman gets up from a fever and the townland says she is changed. The
  narrator, who sold her the ribbon, is the only one who thinks she is
  simply better dressed than they like.
- A soul is doing its purgatory at the back door of the shop where, in
  life, it gave short weight.
