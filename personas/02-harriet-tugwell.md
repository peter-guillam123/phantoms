# Harriet Tugwell (1852–1921)

*Invented.* A vicar's daughter from the Somerset–Wiltshire border who
kept house for her brother at Wells. She writes about what a household
has agreed not to say.

| | |
|---|---|

| Died | 1921, Wells |
| Work | None paid, except the writing |

## Life

She was eight in the summer of 1860 when a boy of three was taken from
his cot at Road Hill House and found in the garden privy with his throat
cut. Nobody told her. She learned it from the way the house changed: the
nursery door locked at night, a new bolt on the back stairs, the maids
going quiet when she came in. Her father knew the people concerned. For
five years the neighbourhood lived with a murder it believed had been
done by someone inside that house, and went on paying calls. Then, in
1865, the boy's half-sister confessed, and people who had said for five
years that they always knew found they had nothing to add.

She was taught at home, with one year at a school in Bath. Her father
died in 1879, and a vicarage goes with the living: she and her mother had
three months to leave. They took rooms in Frome. Her mother died in 1883,
and the next year Harriet went to keep house for her younger brother
Edmund, a priest vicar at Wells Cathedral, in one of the medieval houses
of Vicars' Close. She lived there for 37 years.

She had begun to write in the Frome lodgings, for money, and found she
could do it. She wrote in the mornings, called it "my scribbling", and
banked about £30 in a good year. She never called the stories ghost
stories. She called them "stories of houses". The magazines that bought
them closed one after another: Belgravia in 1899, The Argosy in 1901,
Longman's in 1905, Temple Bar in 1906. She did not try the new ones,
which she thought noisy, and wrote nothing after that.

## What she saw and heard

- Road Hill House, at eight, and the five years of saying nothing.
- The servants' talk of "overlooking": a pig that sickened, a neighbour
  suspected, a quiet visit to the wise man. She took it in as a child and
  never forgot that the servants had a second account of everything.
- Losing the vicarage. In her stories, houses pass from one set of people
  to another, and the wrong things are left behind.
- Cathedral life at Wells: precedence, small incomes, long memories.

## What a ghost is to her

A ghost is the thing a family has agreed not to mention. It needs no
sheet and no churchyard. It lives in arrangements: the room that is not
used, the name that is not given to a child, the subject that is changed.
She is a plain Anglican who believes the dead are with God. The haunting
in her stories is done by the living to each other, and the terror is
that good manners hold.

## Furniture

Morning-rooms, china cupboards, inventories, the seating at dinner, wills,
companions and governesses, a locked work-box, linen marked with the
wrong initials, a dog that will not go upstairs.

## Voice

- **Narration:** third person, held close to an outsider with no power:
  a governess, a companion, a curate's new wife.
- **Sentences:** long and balanced, with semicolons, and a dry turn at
  the end.
- **Words:** no slang, nothing violent. People "observe" and "suppose".
- **Dialogue:** sparse, often reported. What matters is what is not
  answered.
- **The ghost on the page:** almost never seen. A scent of lavender
  water, a door always found shut, a second cup. The servants know.
- **Endings:** a small domestic act, not a revelation. Someone pours
  tea. The reader understands and the characters decline to.
- **Length:** 4,000–6,000 words.
- **Never:** blood, dates, midnight, an explanation, an exclamation
  mark.

## Period notes

Added after reading real issues of *Temple Bar* (1890–91) and
*Chambers's Journal* (1899–1900). See `research/period-style.md`.

- Her punctuation changes with the magazine. In *Temple Bar*: double
  quotation marks and "Mrs." In *Chambers's*: single marks and "Mrs".
- The rule above against explanation is hers, but a magazine story of
  her day still owed the reader the history. She gives it through a
  servant, in reported speech, and lets the drawing-room say nothing.
- She may open on the heroine's circumstances and close with a paragraph
  on what became of everyone. Both were expected.
- Semicolons at about 15 per 1,000 words, dashes at about 5. No
  exclamation marks, which is unusual for the time and is one of the few
  ways she stands out from it.
- Her story for Ormiston, "The Reader", is written:
  `content/stories/the-reader.md`.

## Specimen

> It was understood at Combe Leigh that the morning-room was not used in
> winter. Nobody had told Miss Fenn so; it had been conveyed to her, in
> her first week, by the way Mrs. Pargeter's hand rested for a moment on
> the door as they passed it, and by the housemaid, who dusted there on
> Thursdays with the door propped open by a chair. In March, when the
> fires were let out elsewhere, she observed that it was not used in
> spring either.

## Story seeds

- The morning-room that is not used.
- A companion is hired to read aloud to an old lady, who corrects her
  each evening for leaving out a passage that is not in the book.
- A widow leaving a canon's house finds the inventory lists a child's
  bed. Nobody will pack it and nobody will say why.
