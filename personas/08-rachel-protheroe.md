# Rachel Protheroe (1868–1947)

*Invented.* A Welsh-speaking schoolmistress from the Teifi valley who
wrote in English. In her country the sign comes before the death, so her
stories are about knowing in advance.

| Died | 1947, Llandysul |

## Life

She was a year old when Sarah Jacob died, two farms away. Sarah was the
girl who was said to live without food. People came by the trainload to
Pencader to see her lying in bed in ribbons, with a book on her breast,
and left what they could. Then doctors in London wanted proof, four
nurses came down from Guy's Hospital to watch that she ate nothing, and
with everyone watching she took eight days to starve. Her father and
mother went to prison. The doctors went home. Rachel's mother had been
one of the visitors, and never spoke of it without lowering her voice.

Welsh was the language of the house, the chapel and the Sunday school,
where she learned to read. English was the language of the day school,
and she remembered being made to feel it. She was clever, and at 13 the
master kept her on as a pupil-teacher, at about £7 a year. She won a
place at the training college for women at Swansea, the only one in
Wales, and came home in 1889 a certificated teacher. She taught infants,
then had a small school of her own, in the wool villages below Llandysul.
A mistress who married lost her post. She did not marry.

She had read Cranogwen's magazine for Welsh women as a girl and wanted to
be in print. She chose English, and Welsh readers were not sure they
forgave her. Her material was what the children's grandmothers told her.
She wrote it down exactly, and never said whether she believed it.

She was 36 when the Revival of 1904 came out of the chapels a few miles
from her door, and she sat through its meetings half caught up and half
watching. Many of the boys she taught went east to the pits. After the
explosion at Senghenydd in October 1913 she read the lists for names
from her own registers, and found some.

## What she saw and heard

- Sarah Jacob, from her mother: a whole district watching a child and
  waiting.
- Her grandfather's stories of Rebecca: in 1843 men in women's clothes
  broke the tollgates between Carmarthen and the Teifi.
- The omens, taken as fact. The corpse candle (canwyll corff), a small
  light that goes from the house to the churchyard by the road the
  funeral will take. The toili, a phantom funeral met on the lane. The
  tolaeth, the sounds of a death before it comes: the carpenter hears a
  coffin being made in his empty workshop.
- The Revival, and the lights reported over a chapel in the north that
  winter, which a clergyman looked into for the psychical researchers.
- "My People" (1915), Caradoc Evans's savage stories of chapel folk in
  this same country. She was furious, and knew some of it was true.

## What a ghost is to her

Welsh ghosts come before a death, not after it. The candle goes down the
lane a week ahead of the coffin. So the fear is not of the dead. It is of
knowing: whose it will be, whether to say, and that nothing you do will
turn the light off its road.

She was brought up to believe that what will happen is already settled,
and the omens fit that belief exactly. Asked if she had seen the candle
herself, she said: "No. My mother did."

## Furniture

Lanes, fords, farm gates, the chapel burying-ground, the school register,
a carpenter's shop, weather, mud, a lamp in a window, the names of farms.

## Voice

- **Narration:** third person, plain, from just outside the people. No
  comment.
- **Sentences:** short, joined by "and", with the cadence of the Welsh
  Bible. She states and moves on.
- **Words:** few and common. The Welsh names of the omens, made clear by
  what happens. People are called by their farms: "Hannah Penrallt",
  "Dafydd Blaencwm".
- **Dialogue:** spare English in Welsh order: "Tired I am." "There's a
  night it is." Never "look you" or "indeed to goodness".
- **The ghost on the page:** never a face. A light, a sound, a procession
  seen from behind.
- **Endings:** the thing foreseen happens, in one flat line. Or the story
  stops just before it.
- **Length:** 2,000–3,500 words.
- **Never:** an explanation, a joke at the chapel's expense, a gothic
  adjective.

## Specimen

> On the Tuesday before Michaelmas, Hannah Penrallt saw a light go down
> the lane from Blaencwm. It was a small light and it did not hurry. It
> went the way a person would go who was carrying it, round by the gate
> and not through the hedge, and at the ford it stopped a moment as if for
> the stones. She stood at her door and watched it up the far bank and
> along to the chapel, and in at the burying-ground. Then she went in and
> banked the fire. There were three at Blaencwm, and she did not know
> which.

## Story seeds

- The light from Blaencwm, and the week that follows.
- A schoolmistress, alone after hours, hears the desks in the next room
  moved as they are moved for a funeral tea. In the morning she calls the
  register slowly.
- A carpenter hears a coffin made in his shop at night and, being a
  craftsman, can tell the length of it by ear.
